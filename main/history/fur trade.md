# fur trade

![[Tableau_de_Troc_fourrures.jpg]]

## Overview

The **fur trade** was one of the most important economic activities in colonial North America, driving European exploration, shaping relationships between European powers and Native Americans, and laying the groundwork for European colonization. From the early 1600s through the early 1800s, the trade in beaver pelts and other furs connected Native hunters to European markets and fundamentally transformed indigenous life.

## European Competition

France, England, and the Netherlands competed fiercely for control of the fur trade. The French built an especially extensive network of alliances with Native peoples, trading metal goods, textiles, and firearms for beaver pelts destined for European hat makers. The Dutch traded from **New Amsterdam** (New York). The British operated through the **Hudson's Bay Company** (founded 1670), which controlled vast Canadian territories.

## Impact on Native Americans

The fur trade created both opportunities and disruptions for Native peoples. Access to European goods (metal tools, guns) gave some tribes significant advantages, but dependency on trade goods, epidemic diseases spread through contact, and competition over hunting grounds destabilized many Native societies.

## Connection to Lewis and Clark

When [[Thomas Jefferson]] sent the **[[Lewis and Clark Expedition]]** west (1804–1806), one of his goals was to establish American presence in the fur trade of the Pacific Northwest.
