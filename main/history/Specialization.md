# Specialization

## Overview

**Specialization** in economics refers to the practice of focusing production on a specific good or service rather than attempting to produce everything needed for subsistence. The shift from **[[subsistence farmers|subsistence farming]]** to specialized commercial agriculture and manufacturing was a defining feature of the **[[Market Revolution]]** in early 19th-century America.

## From Subsistence to Specialization

Colonial American farmers typically grew a variety of crops and raised multiple types of livestock to meet their own needs. As transportation improved (**[[Erie Canal]]**, [[Railroad|railroads]]), farmers could sell surpluses to distant markets. This created incentives to specialize — grow only wheat, or only cotton — and purchase other necessities with cash.

## Industrial Specialization

In factories, the **division of labor** — breaking production into specialized tasks performed by different workers — dramatically increased productivity. The **Lowell mills** exemplified this: workers performed single, repetitive tasks rather than making an entire garment from scratch.

## Economic Benefits and Costs

Specialization increased overall economic output and lowered prices for goods, but it also created dependence on markets and made workers vulnerable to economic downturns. A subsistence farmer could survive a recession; a factory worker or cash-crop farmer could not.
