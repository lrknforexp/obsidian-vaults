> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Date:** December 27, 1512
> **Issued by:** King Ferdinand II of Spain
> **Location of Issuance:** Burgos, Spain
> **Purpose:** First attempt to regulate Spanish treatment of Native Americans in the colonies
> **Significance:** First body of European law governing relations with indigenous peoples in the Americas

---

The **Law of Burgos** (1512) was the first formal Spanish legal code governing the treatment of **Native Americans** in the colonies of [[New Spain]]. Issued by King Ferdinand II, it attempted to codify and nominally limit the abuses of the **[[Encomienda System]]** while simultaneously affirming the right of Spaniards to use Native labor.

---

## Background

By 1512, Dominican friars — particularly Friar Antonio de Montesinos — had raised loud moral objections to the brutal treatment of Native Americans under the encomienda system in the Caribbean. His sermons accused Spanish colonists of mortal sin for the way they treated indigenous people. The Spanish Crown convened a council of lawyers and theologians at Burgos to respond.

---

## Key Provisions

> [!info] What the Law Required
> - Encomenderos holding more than **50 Native workers** must educate **one boy** in reading, writing, and Christian doctrine to become a future teacher
> - Native workers must be given **adequate food, clothing, and housing**
> - Native people must not be called **"dogs"** or other demeaning names
> - Native women who were pregnant could not be sent to work in the mines
> - Native workers must be allowed to **rest on Sundays** and feast days
> - Encomenderos must build **churches** and provide religious instruction

---

## What the Law Revealed

The very existence of the Law of Burgos revealed the moral contradictions at the heart of the Spanish colonial project:
- It acknowledged that Native Americans were being brutally abused
- It affirmed that they were human beings entitled to some minimal protections
- It simultaneously legitimized the [[Encomienda System]] and Spanish authority over Native peoples
- Its enforcement was virtually nonexistent — the colonial frontier was thousands of miles from Spanish courts

> [!info] Impact
> The Law of Burgos was among the **first human rights legislation** in history — and among the first to be systematically ignored. Abuse of Native Americans continued largely unchanged. A further law, the **New Laws of 1542**, attempted to abolish the encomienda system outright — and sparked a near-revolt among colonial encomenderos that forced the Crown to back down.
