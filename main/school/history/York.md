> [!info] Quick Facts

> **Significance:** First African American to cross the North American continent; full participant in the [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]]

---

**York** was an enslaved man owned by explorer **[[William Clark]]** who accompanied the **[[Lewis and Clark Expedition]]** (1804–1806), becoming the **first African American to cross the North American continent**. He was a full and active participant in the Corps of Discovery — doing the same work, facing the same dangers, and contributing the same skills as any other member of the expedition.

---

## Role on the Expedition

York was not a servant on the periphery of the expedition. He:
- Hunted and gathered food
- Performed physical labor alongside other crew members
- Participated in **diplomatic encounters** with Native peoples, who were often fascinated by his appearance and physical strength — many Native peoples had never seen a Black man before
- **Voted** on at least one critical group decision — where to build their winter camp at the Pacific Coast (Fort Clatsop). This makes him one of the first African Americans to vote in a group decision in American history.

---

## Treatment After the Expedition

Despite his indispensable contributions, York returned from the expedition to the status of enslaved property:
- He asked Clark for his freedom — repeatedly
- Clark refused for years, calling York "troublesome" for his continued requests for freedom
- Clark eventually freed York around **1811** (accounts vary), reportedly setting him up as a freight hauler in Tennessee
- His ultimate fate is uncertain — some accounts say he died of cholera before 1832; a fur trader claimed to have met a man matching York's description living with the Crow Nation in the 1830s

---

## Legacy

York's story highlights the profound contradiction at the heart of the Lewis and Clark Expedition — an enterprise launched by slave-owning founders to expand a democracy that excluded the people who built it. His full participation, bravery, and skills were equal to any member of the Corps; his freedom was denied for years after.
