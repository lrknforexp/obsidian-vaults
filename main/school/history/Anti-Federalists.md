# Anti-Federalists

## Overview

The **Anti-Federalists** were a loosely organized political movement that opposed ratification of the U.S. Constitution in 1787–1788. They feared that the new Constitution created a central government that was too powerful, threatened states' rights, and lacked adequate protections for individual liberties.

## Key Concerns

Anti-Federalists warned that a strong central government would eventually become tyrannical, echoing their recent experience under British rule. Their most specific objection was the **absence of a Bill of Rights** — without explicit guarantees of free speech, religion, and due process, they argued the federal government could trample individual freedoms.

## Key Figures

Prominent Anti-Federalists included **[[Patrick Henry]]**, **[[George Mason]]** (who refused to sign the Constitution), **[[George Clinton]]** of New York, and the authors of the *Brutus* essays. They published pamphlets and argued passionately in state ratification conventions.

## The Federalist Response

[[Alexander Hamilton]], [[James Madison]], and [[John Jay]] wrote the **[[Federalist Papers]]** directly responding to Anti-Federalist arguments. Madison's Federalist No. 51 addressed fears of tyranny through checks and balances.

## Legacy

Though they lost the ratification debate, Anti-Federalists won a crucial concession: the promise that a **[[Bill of Rights]]** would be added immediately after ratification, which Madison delivered in 1791.
