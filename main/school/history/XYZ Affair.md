---

The **XYZ Affair** was a 1797–1798 diplomatic incident in which French agents demanded bribes from American peace envoys before negotiations could even begin. When President [[John Adams]] revealed the details to Congress, public outrage swept the nation and set the stage for the undeclared naval conflict known as the **[[Quasi-War]]**.

---

## Background

Relations between the U.S. and France had deteriorated sharply after **[[Jay's Treaty]]** (1794), which France saw as a pro-British betrayal of the Franco-American alliance. French naval vessels began seizing American merchant ships in the Caribbean.

President [[John Adams]] sent three envoys to Paris to negotiate a settlement: **[[Charles Cotesworth Pinckney]]**, **[[John Marshall]]**, and **[[Elbridge Gerry]]**.

---

## The Affair

> [!info] The Demand for Bribes
> When the American envoys arrived in Paris, French Foreign Minister **Talleyrand** refused to meet with them directly. Instead, his agents — referred to in the published American dispatches only as **"X," "Y," and "Z"** (to protect their identities) — approached the Americans with a list of demands:
>
> 1. A **bribe of $250,000** paid directly to Talleyrand personally
> 2. A **$10 million loan** from the United States to France
> 3. A formal **apology** from Adams for anti-French remarks he had made
>
> Only after these payments would Talleyrand even agree to negotiate.

The Americans refused. Pinckney's reported response became famous: **"No! No! Not a sixpence!"**

---

## American Reaction

Adams initially kept the dispatches secret, but when Congress demanded to see them, he released the full account. The public reaction was explosive:

> [!info] "Millions for Defense, But Not One Cent for Tribute!"
> The slogan — actually coined by South Carolina politician Robert Harper — swept the country. Americans were furious that France had treated their diplomats with such contempt. War fever gripped the nation.
>
> The [[Federalist Party]] used the crisis to push through the **[[Alien and Sedition Acts]]** and to begin building up the military. Congress authorized the creation of a permanent Navy and expansion of the Army.

---

## Consequences

- Directly caused the **[[Quasi-War]]** — an undeclared naval conflict with France (1798–1800)
- Led to the **[[Alien and Sedition Acts]]** — the Federalist-controlled Congress used war fever to suppress dissent
- Led to the creation of the **[[Department of the Navy]]** and re-establishment of the [[U.S. Marine Corps]]
- Was a **political windfall** for Adams and the Federalists — briefly
- Eventually contributed to Adams's defeat in the [[Election of 1800]] when he pursued peace over war

---

## Elbridge Gerry's Role

**[[Elbridge Gerry]]** was the most sympathetic to France of the three envoys and stayed in Paris after Pinckney and Marshall left. His willingness to continue talking with the French despite the insults created political controversy at home. He later became Vice President under [[James Madison]] and is remembered today primarily for the gerrymander — a redistricting manipulation named after him.
