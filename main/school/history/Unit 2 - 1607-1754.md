## The Atlantic Slave Trade and the Middle Passage

The **[[Middle Passage]]** was the brutal transatlantic leg of the [[Triangular Trade]] — the forced sea journey enslaved Africans were made to endure from West Africa to the Americas. It was one of the most horrific chapters in human history, defined by extreme cruelty and staggeringly high death rates.

> [!info] The Triangular Trade
> The **[[Triangular Trade]]** was a three-legged commercial system:
> 1. **Europe → Africa**: manufactured goods (guns, textiles, rum)
> 2. **Africa → Americas**: enslaved people *(the Middle Passage)*
> 3. **Americas → Europe**: raw materials (tobacco, sugar, cotton)
> 
> The Middle Passage was the second leg — and the most deadly.

---

## Conditions of the Middle Passage

The conditions aboard slave ships were deliberately dehumanizing. Enslaved people were treated as cargo, not human beings:

| Condition | Details |
|---|---|
| Overcrowding | People were **packed tightly** into the ship's hold with almost no room to move |
| Restraints | Captives were **chained together** for most of the voyage |
| Deprivation | Very little food, water, or space was provided |
| Disease | Illness spread rapidly in the overcrowded, unsanitary conditions |
| Violence | Crew used **whips, restraints, and physical abuse** to control enslaved people |
| Duration | Journeys lasted **6–10 weeks** across the Atlantic |

> [!info] Death Toll
> The mortality rate during the Middle Passage was catastrophically high. Disease, starvation, and abuse were routine. For slave traders, the goal was **profit** — human life and safety were secondary concerns at best.

### Resistance at Sea

Revolts did occur aboard slave ships, though they were rare. The reasons were significant:
- Enslaved people were **physically weakened** from starvation and deprivation
- They were **outnumbered** and heavily armed crews stood against them
- Captives were **chained**, limiting their ability to coordinate

Despite these obstacles, some enslaved Africans fought back — a testament to the human will to resist even under the most oppressive conditions.

## The First Great Awakening

**Period:** 1730s–1740s

The **[[First Great Awakening]]** was a sweeping religious revival movement that transformed colonial American society. It challenged the established social and religious order, encouraging ordinary people — regardless of class or background — to seek a direct, personal relationship with God.

> [!info] What was the Great Awakening?
> The Great Awakening was a Protestant religious revival characterized by:
> - **Evangelicalism** — an emphasis on personal conversion and emotional, passionate preaching
> - The concept of **"new birth"** — the idea that a transformative spiritual experience (being "born again") was the pinnacle of religious life
> - The belief that **anyone** could be saved, not just those approved by traditional church hierarchy

---

## Old Lights vs. New Lights

The Great Awakening created a deep divide in colonial religious life between those who embraced the revival and those who rejected it:

| | Old Lights | New Lights |
|---|---|---|
| **Position** | Opposed the revival | Embraced the revival |
| **Churches** | [[Congregationalists]], [[Anglicans]] | [[Presbyterians]], [[Methodists]], [[Baptists]] |
| **View on authority** | Believed traditional church hierarchy was necessary | Believed anyone could convert; no need for institutional gatekeeping |
| **Tone** | Formal, structured, reserved | Emotional, passionate, populist |

> [!info] Why It Mattered
> The Great Awakening was more than a religious movement — it was a **social and political disruption**. By insisting that any person could experience God directly, it challenged the authority of established institutions and the social hierarchies that supported them. Many historians see it as an early precursor to the democratic spirit of the [[American Revolution]].

---

## Leaders of the Great Awakening

### [[George Whitefield]]
An English Anglican minister who became the most famous preacher of the revival. Whitefield traveled throughout the colonies delivering electrifying open-air sermons to enormous crowds, helping spread the revival from Georgia to New England.

### [[Jonathan Edwards]]
A Congregationalist minister based in Massachusetts, best known for his fire-and-brimstone sermon *[[Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God]]* (1741). Edwards combined emotional preaching with rigorous theological reasoning, making him one of the most intellectually significant figures of the revival.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| [[Middle Passage]] | The transatlantic leg of the triangular trade during which enslaved Africans were transported to the Americas |
| [[Triangular Trade]] | The three-legged transatlantic trade system connecting Europe, Africa, and the Americas |
| [[First Great Awakening]] | A Protestant religious revival in the American colonies during the 1730s–1740s |
| [[Evangelicalism]] | A Protestant movement emphasizing personal conversion, scripture, and passionate preaching |
| [[George Whitefield]] | English minister and leading figure of the Great Awakening |
| [[Jonathan Edwards]] | American Congregationalist minister and theologian of the Great Awakening |
| Old Lights | Colonial churches that opposed the Great Awakening (Congregationalists, Anglicans) |
| New Lights | Colonial churches that embraced the Great Awakening (Presbyterians, Methodists, Baptists) |
