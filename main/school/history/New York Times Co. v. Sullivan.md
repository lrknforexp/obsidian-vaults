# New York Times Co. v. Sullivan

## Overview

**New York Times Co. v. Sullivan** (1964) was a landmark Supreme Court decision that established crucial protections for freedom of the press under the **[[First Amendment]]**. The Court ruled that public officials cannot successfully sue for defamation unless they prove the publisher acted with **"actual malice"** — knowing the statement was false or acting with reckless disregard for its truth or falsity.

## Background

During the Civil Rights Movement, Alabama Public Safety Commissioner L.B. Sullivan sued the *New York Times* over a full-page advertisement that contained minor factual errors in describing police treatment of civil rights protesters. An Alabama jury awarded Sullivan $500,000 in damages.

## The Supreme Court's Ruling

Justice William Brennan wrote that the First Amendment requires a free press to be able to criticize public officials vigorously — even imperfectly — without facing financial ruin. A rule requiring absolute factual accuracy would cause newspapers to self-censor legitimate criticism of government.

## The "Actual Malice" Standard

To protect press freedom, public officials must prove the defendant knew the statement was false OR acted with reckless disregard for whether it was true — a very high bar. This standard still governs defamation law for public officials and public figures.

## Significance

Sullivan is considered the most important First Amendment case in American history, making robust press coverage of government actions legally safe and enabling the investigative journalism that holds power accountable.
