# Evangelicalism

## Overview

**Evangelicalism** is a Protestant Christian movement emphasizing the need for personal conversion ("being born again"), the authority of the Bible, the importance of spreading the faith (evangelism), and the centrality of Christ's atonement. It has been one of the most powerful forces in American religious and social life from the colonial era to the present.

## Colonial Roots

American evangelicalism took shape during the **[[First Great Awakening]]** (1730s–1740s), when preachers like **[[George Whitefield]]** and **[[Jonathan Edwards]]** sparked mass religious revivals emphasizing emotional conversion experiences over cold doctrinal formalism.

## Second Great Awakening

The **[[Second Great Awakening]]** (1790s–1840s) expanded evangelicalism dramatically, creating new denominations (Methodist, Baptist), new institutions (revivals, camp meetings), and linking evangelical faith to social reform.

## Social Reform

Evangelical conviction drove many 19th-century reform movements: **abolition** (many abolitionists were evangelical Christians who believed slavery was a sin), **temperance**, **prison reform**, and **women's rights**. The Second Great Awakening created the moral energy behind much of antebellum reform.

## Baptist and Methodist Growth

The Baptist and Methodist denominations grew explosively during this period because their democratic theology (salvation available to all), energetic preaching, and lay participation appealed to ordinary Americans in ways that older, more formal denominations did not.
