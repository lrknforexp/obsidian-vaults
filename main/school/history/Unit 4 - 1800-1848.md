## Washington's Presidency — Highlights

**[[George Washington]]** served as the first president of the United States, setting precedents that shaped the office for generations.

> [!info] Washington's Key Precedents
> - **Two-term limit** — Washington voluntarily stepped down after two terms, establishing the tradition that held until [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] and was later codified by the [[22nd Amendment]]
> - **Presidential authority** — Demonstrated through the [[Whiskey Rebellion]] that the federal government could and would enforce its laws by force
> - **Foreign policy neutrality** — Warned against permanent alliances in his [[Farewell Address]]

### The Whiskey Rebellion (1794)

> [!info] The Whiskey Rebellion
> When Congress imposed an excise tax on whiskey as part of [[Alexander Hamilton]]'s plan to reduce national debt, farmers in western Pennsylvania — many of whom distilled whiskey as their primary income — revolted.
>
> Washington personally led federal troops to suppress the rebellion. It was the **first major test** of federal authority and a demonstration that the new government could enforce its laws in a way the old [[Articles of Confederation]] never could.

### Washington's Farewell Address (1796)

In his farewell address, Washington issued three major warnings:
1. **Against political parties** — he feared they would divide and weaken the nation
2. **Against sectionalism** — regional infighting would tear the country apart
3. **Against foreign entanglements** — permanent alliances with foreign nations would drag America into unnecessary wars

> [!info] Jay's Treaty (1794)
> **[[Jay's Treaty]]** established that the **president is the chief foreign diplomat**, responsible for negotiating treaties with foreign powers (subject to Senate ratification). It resolved lingering conflicts with Britain but proved deeply controversial.

## The First Political Parties

Despite Washington's warnings, **political parties emerged almost immediately** — driven largely by debate over Hamilton's economic program and the proper interpretation of the Constitution.

| | [[Federalist Party]] | [[Democratic-Republican Party]] |
|---|---|---|
| **Leaders** | [[Alexander Hamilton]], [[John Adams]] | [[Thomas Jefferson]], [[James Madison]] |
| **Constitutional interpretation** | **Loose** — the government has *implied powers* beyond what's explicitly written | **Strict** — the government can only do what the Constitution explicitly says |
| **View on government** | Wanted a **strong central government** | Favored **states' rights** and a weak national government |
| **Hamilton's economic program** | **Favored** — seen as beneficial to economic growth | **Opposed** — seen as giving too much power to the federal government |

> [!info] Hamilton's Economic Program
> [[Alexander Hamilton]]'s financial plan included:
> - Assumption of state war debts by the federal government
> - Creation of a **[[National Bank]]**
> - Protective tariffs to encourage domestic manufacturing
> - Excise taxes (including the one that sparked the Whiskey Rebellion)
>
> Jefferson and Madison fiercely opposed these measures, arguing they exceeded federal authority and benefited wealthy merchants and bankers at the expense of farmers.

## The Alien and Sedition Acts

**[[John Adams]]** was elected president in **1797**, inheriting a crisis with France.

### The Quasi-War (1798–1800)

> [!info] Quasi-War
> The **[[Quasi-War]]** was an undeclared, unofficial naval conflict between the United States and France. France was angered by [[Jay's Treaty]], which it viewed as pro-British, and began seizing American merchant ships in the Caribbean and Atlantic. American and French naval vessels fought throughout the period without a formal declaration of war.

### The Acts

Congress responded by passing **four laws** known collectively as the **[[Alien and Sedition Acts]]**:

| Act | Effect |
|---|---|
| Naturalization Act | Extended the residency requirement for citizenship from 5 to 14 years |
| Alien Friends Act | Allowed the president to **deport** any non-citizen deemed dangerous |
| Alien Enemies Act | Allowed the president to **imprison or deport** citizens of enemy nations |
| **Sedition Act** | Made it illegal to publish **"false, scandalous, or malicious"** criticism of the government |

> [!info] Constitutional Crisis
> The Alien and Sedition Acts triggered a major debate about whether they violated the **[[First Amendment]]** and the **[[Bill of Rights]]**. Jefferson and Madison responded with the **[[Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions]]**, arguing that states had the right to **nullify** unconstitutional federal laws — a doctrine that would resurface dramatically before the [[Civil War]].
>
> The acts were either allowed to expire or repealed after Adams's presidency ended.

## Marbury v. Madison

**[[Thomas Jefferson]]** was elected president in **1800** in what he called the "Revolution of 1800" — the first peaceful transfer of power between rival parties in American history.

In the final days of Adams's presidency, he rushed to appoint as many Federalist judges as possible — the so-called **"[[midnight judges]]."** One appointee, **[[William Marbury]]**, never received his official commission.

> [!info] Marbury v. Madison (1803)
> Marbury sued Secretary of State **[[James Madison]]** to force delivery of his commission. Chief Justice **[[John Marshall]]** used the case to establish one of the most important principles in American constitutional law:
>
> **[[Judicial Review]]** — the power of the Supreme Court to declare acts of Congress or the executive branch **unconstitutional**.
>
> The Constitution does not explicitly grant this power, but Marshall argued it was **implied** by the structure of constitutional government. It remains the foundation of judicial authority in the United States.

## The Louisiana Purchase

In **1803**, French Emperor **[[Napoleon Bonaparte]]** — facing war in Europe and the collapse of French ambitions in the Americas — offered to sell the vast **[[Louisiana Territory]]** to the United States.

> [!info] The Louisiana Purchase
> - **Price:** $15 million
> - **Result:** The United States **doubled in size** overnight
> - **Jefferson's dilemma:** Jefferson believed in strict constitutional interpretation, but the Constitution said nothing about buying foreign territory. He ultimately set aside his principles, arguing the purchase was too important to pass up.

### Lewis and Clark Expedition

Jefferson immediately sent **[[Meriwether Lewis]]** and **[[William Clark]]** to explore and map the new territory.

> [!info] The Expedition (1804–1806)
> - **[[Sacagawea]]**, a [[Shoshone]] woman, traveled with the expedition alongside her newborn son, serving as a translator and guide
> - She helped **secure food, supplies, and horses** from Native tribes
> - She **identified plant life** and helped navigate unfamiliar terrain
> - She **prevented conflict** with several Native American tribes
> - Relations with Native peoples were generally friendly, though explorers held patronizing views of indigenous peoples

**Results:**
- Mapped routes from [[St. Louis]] to the [[Pacific Ocean]]
- Documented rivers, mountains, plants, and animals
- Returned in 1806 after nearly three years

## The Missouri Compromise

The addition of the Louisiana Territory raised an urgent question: would new states carved from this land be **slave states** or **free states**?

### The Crisis

At the time, the number of slave and free states in the Senate was **perfectly balanced** — 11 each. Adding new states threatened to tip the balance dramatically in one direction.

> [!info] The Missouri Compromise (1820)
> Crafted primarily by **[[Henry Clay]]**, the compromise established:
> 1. **[[Missouri]]** was admitted as a **slave state**
> 2. **[[Maine]]** was admitted as a **free state** (preserving the balance)
> 3. A geographic line was drawn at **[[36°30' latitude]]** — all new states **north** of this line would be **free**, all states **south** would be **slave**

| Territory | Status |
|---|---|
| Missouri | Slave state |
| Maine | Free state |
| North of 36°30' | Free territory |
| South of 36°30' | Slave territory |

> [!info] Significance
> The Missouri Compromise **kept the peace** for a generation, but it did not solve the underlying problem. It established the dangerous precedent of drawing geographic lines around slavery. The compromise would ultimately be undone by the [[Kansas-Nebraska Act]] of 1854.

## The Industrial Revolution

> [!info] What Was the Industrial Revolution?
> The **[[Industrial Revolution]]** was an effort to increase production by using **machines** rather than humans or animals. It began in **[[Great Britain]]** in the **1700s** and soon spread to the United States. **[[Steam engines]]** gradually replaced the work previously done by men and animals, fundamentally reshaping the economy and society.

### The Artisan Economy

Before factories, goods were made by hand in one of two settings:

**Artisan Workshops**

An **[[artisan]]** was a skilled craftsman — a blacksmith, cobbler, cooper, wheelmaker, tailor, and so on:
- Every product was **crafted entirely by hand** from start to finish
- Items were made as **one complete piece** with no individual parts
- The process was **difficult and extremely time-consuming**

**The Cottage Industry**

> [!info] Cottage Industry
> The **[[cottage industry]]** was a system in which companies sent raw materials to workers' **homes**, where women would sew or assemble products and send them back in exchange for a commission. Production happened in private homes, not centralized factories.

## Life Before the Market Revolution

Before the [[Market Revolution]], most Americans lived in **rural environments** and operated as **[[subsistence farmers]]** — producing what they needed largely for themselves:

- Grew food and raised animals **for their own consumption**
- Made other necessities at home — clothes, furniture, tools
- Sold **excess goods** to earn limited cash income
- **Bartered** with neighbors for things they could not produce themselves

> [!info] The Barter Economy
> In a **[[barter economy]]**, goods and services are exchanged directly without money changing hands. Most early American farmers operated this way — trading surplus crops or handmade goods with neighbors rather than buying and selling in a commercial marketplace.

---

## The Market Revolution

The **[[Market Revolution]]** was a sweeping transformation of the American economy in the first half of the 19th century — shifting Americans away from subsistence farming and toward participating in a national commercial marketplace.

> [!info] What Is a Market?
> A **market** is any system or place where buyers and sellers exchange goods and services. Examples include a local grocery store, a farmer's market, the [[New York Stock Exchange]], or an online retailer. During the Market Revolution, far more Americans became participants in **larger market economies**, purchasing from others rather than making everything themselves.

### Key Drivers of the Market Revolution

**[[Specialization]]** — focusing on producing **1 or 2 products or crops** rather than everything:
- Instead of making everything themselves, people produced what they were best at and **bought the rest**
- Specialization made the Market Revolution possible and necessary

**[[Free Enterprise]]** — the economic system that fueled growth:
- Private businesses **control the means of production** and earn profits
- Growth is driven by competition and consumer demand rather than government direction

**[[Entrepreneurs]]** — investors who **risked their own capital** in new industries:
- Willingness to take financial risk was essential to building the new industrial economy

### Causes and Results

| Causes | Results |
|---|---|
| Improvements in **transportation** | **Increased commercialization** — more Americans buying and selling goods |
| Increased **industrialization** | Surge in **manufacturing** (especially in the North and Northwest) |
| Improvements in **communications** | National markets connected; regions became interdependent |

## Transportation Improvements

Efficient transportation was the backbone of the Market Revolution. Moving products became **as important as making them** — manufacturers needed reliable ways to get goods from factory to consumer quickly and cheaply.

### Steamboats

> [!info] Robert Fulton and the Steamboat
> **[[Robert Fulton]]** invented the practical steamboat, which could travel both **upriver and downriver** — something sail-powered vessels could not reliably do. Steamboats made river shipping **faster, easier, and significantly cheaper**, opening up the vast interior river system to commercial trade.

### Canals

> [!info] The Erie Canal
> **[[Canals]]** are man-made waterways that extend the reach of river transportation to inland areas not naturally connected to rivers, lakes, or oceans. The most famous was the **[[Erie Canal]]** (completed 1825), which linked the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and New York City — connecting the **Northeast and Northwest** and dramatically cutting shipping costs.
>
> Canals allowed goods to move between regions that had previously been commercially isolated.

### Railroads

> [!info] The Rise of the Railroad
> The **[[railroad]]** was the most transformative transportation innovation of the era, enabling **overland movement of both people and products** at speeds previously impossible.
>
> | Year | Miles of Railroad Track in the U.S. |
> |---|---|
> | 1830 | 13 miles |
> | 1860 | 31,000 miles |
>
> **Advantages of railroads over other transportation:**
> - Could operate **year-round**, including in winter when rivers and canals froze
> - Brought goods **further inland**, far from rivers and coastlines
> - Were dramatically **faster** than wagons, canal boats, or even steamboats

> [!info] Why Transportation Mattered
> Improved transportation made America's regions **economically interdependent** for the first time. Efficient transportation also became a **competitive necessity** — manufacturers who couldn't move their goods quickly couldn't compete. Moving products became as strategically important as producing them.

---

## The Telegraph and Communications

> [!info] Samuel F.B. Morse and the Telegraph
> **[[Samuel F.B. Morse]]** invented the **[[telegraph]]** — a device capable of transmitting messages across vast distances through electrical signals and his famous **[[Morse Code]]**.
>
> The telegraph's impact was revolutionary:
> - Provided **instant communication** across the entire nation — North, South, and West
> - Allowed companies and consumers to discuss products, prices, and orders in real time
> - Transformed the speed at which business, news, and information moved throughout the country
> - Functionally **shrank the size of the nation** by collapsing the time needed to communicate

## Regional Specialization

As the Market Revolution matured, the three major regions of the United States developed distinct economic identities and became increasingly dependent on one another:

| Region | Economic Identity | Key Products |
|---|---|---|
| **South** | Cash crop agriculture | Tobacco, cotton, rice |
| **Northeast** | Commerce and manufacturing | Factory goods, textiles, finance |
| **Midwest** | Agricultural specialization | Wheat, corn, cattle |

> [!info] Regional Interdependence
> Thanks to railroads, steamboats, and canals, **all three regions began trading with one another** on a true national scale for the first time. The South supplied raw cotton to Northern mills; the Midwest sent grain and livestock to Eastern cities; the Northeast sold manufactured goods to farmers across the country. No region was economically self-sufficient any longer.

## Social Changes of the Market Revolution

The Market Revolution transformed American **society** as profoundly as it transformed the economy.

### Urbanization

> [!info] Urban Growth
> As factories multiplied, Americans left rural farms and migrated to **cities** in search of factory wages. This process — **[[urbanization]]** — concentrated populations in industrial centers like [[Lowell, Massachusetts]], [[New York City]], and [[Philadelphia]]. Cities grew rapidly and often chaotically, struggling to provide housing, sanitation, and services for the flood of new arrivals.

### Changes in Labor

The factory system created enormous pressure on workers:

- Manufacturers needed **cheap labor** for factory work
- Competition forced factory owners to **cut wages** and allow working conditions to deteriorate
  - Lower wages → more profit
  - Cheaper prices → more sales
  - All centered on maintaining profit margins
- Factory owners increasingly turned to **[[immigrant labor]]** — particularly the **[[Irish]]**, who arrived in massive numbers following the [[Great Famine]] of the 1840s

> [!info] Labor Conditions
> The relentless pressure to cut costs meant that factory workers — many of them women and children — faced **long hours, dangerous conditions, and poverty wages**. This exploitation would eventually give rise to the American **[[labor movement]]** and organized unions later in the 19th century.

## Westward Expansion

From **1800 to 1850**, the United States expanded dramatically westward through a combination of ideology, land acquisition, and military force.

> [!info] Manifest Destiny
> **[[Manifest Destiny]]** was the widespread belief that the United States was **destined by God** to expand across the entire North American continent, from the Atlantic to the Pacific Ocean. This ideology justified land acquisitions, the [[Mexican-American War]], and the violent displacement of Native American peoples.

### The Link Between Expansion and Industrialization

Industrial inventions and westward expansion were inseparable — each made the other possible:

> [!info] Why Both Needed Each Other
> - **Without industrial inventions**, America could not meaningfully move west — there was no practical way to transport people, goods, or armies across thousands of miles of wilderness
> - **Without westward expansion**, the Market Revolution had limited room to grow — new territories provided raw materials, agricultural land, and new consumer markets
> - The two forces **fueled each other**: expansion opened new economic opportunities; industrialization provided the tools to exploit them

### Major Causes of Expansion (1800–1848)

| Cause | Details |
|---|---|
| **[[Manifest Destiny]]** | The belief that American continental expansion was God-given and inevitable |
| **[[Louisiana Purchase]] (1803)** | Doubled U.S. territory overnight for $15 million |
| **[[Adams-Onís Treaty]] (1819)** | Acquired Florida from Spain |
| **[[Texas Annexation]] (1845)** | Republic of Texas admitted as a state, provoking conflict with Mexico |
| **[[Mexican-American War]] (1846–1848)** | War with Mexico resulted in the U.S. acquiring California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, and more |
