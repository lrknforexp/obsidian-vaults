# Immigrant labor

## Overview

**Immigrant labor** was a driving force behind American industrial and economic growth throughout the 19th century. Waves of immigrants — first from Germany and Ireland in the 1840s–1850s, then later from Southern and Eastern Europe — provided cheap, abundant labor for factories, construction projects, and agriculture.

## Irish Immigration

The **[[Great Famine]]** (1845–1852) in Ireland drove approximately one million Irish immigrants to America. Desperately poor and facing discrimination ("No Irish Need Apply"), Irish immigrants took the most dangerous and lowest-paid jobs: digging canals, building **[[Railroad|railroads]]**, and working in textile mills.

## German Immigration

German immigrants of the 1840s–1850s (many fleeing the failed revolutions of 1848) were often more skilled and prosperous, settling in the Midwest and working as farmers, craftsmen, and businessmen.

## Impact on Labor

Immigrant workers swelled the industrial workforce, but their willingness to work for low wages (driven by desperate poverty) often undercut native-born workers' wages and fueled tensions. Employers sometimes used new immigrants to break strikes.

## Nativism

The flood of Catholic immigrants (Irish, German) alarmed Protestant native-born Americans, sparking the **Know-Nothing Party** movement of the 1850s, which sought to restrict immigration and limit immigrants' political rights.
