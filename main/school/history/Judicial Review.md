# Judicial Review

## Overview

**Judicial review** is the power of federal courts — especially the Supreme Court — to declare laws passed by Congress or actions of the executive branch **unconstitutional** and therefore void. Though not explicitly stated in the Constitution, it was established by Chief Justice **[[John Marshall]]** in the landmark case **[[Marbury v. Madison]]** (1803).

## Constitutional Basis

The Constitution does not explicitly grant courts the power of judicial review. The [[Federalist Papers]] (No. 78, by [[Alexander Hamilton]]) argued that the judiciary must have the power to void laws that violate the Constitution — otherwise the Constitution would be meaningless.

## Marbury v. Madison

When President [[Thomas Jefferson]] refused to deliver William Marbury's judicial commission (signed by the outgoing President [[John Adams]]), Marbury sued. Marshall ruled that while Marbury deserved his commission, the law under which he sued was unconstitutional — thus establishing the Supreme Court's power to void acts of Congress.

## Significance

Judicial review made the Supreme Court the ultimate interpreter of the Constitution and created the system of **checks and balances** between branches. It remains one of the most important principles in American constitutional law.
