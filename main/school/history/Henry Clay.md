**Henry Clay** was one of the most influential American politicians of the 19th century — a towering figure who shaped national policy for over four decades. Known as **"The Great Compromiser"**, he crafted major legislative deals that held the Union together during the dangerous debates over slavery and sectionalism. He ran for president three times and lost all three, but his influence on American policy — particularly his **American System** of economic nationalism — was enormous.

---

## Early Career

Clay was born in Virginia but made his career in Kentucky, becoming one of that state's most successful lawyers and politicians. He was elected to the **U.S. Senate** while still too young to legally serve (he was 29; the minimum age is 30) and later became **Speaker of the House** — one of the most powerful legislative positions in the country.

---

## The American System

Clay's most significant policy legacy was the **American System** — a comprehensive economic program of:

> [!info] The American System
> 1. **Protective tariffs** — taxes on imported goods to protect American manufacturers from foreign competition
> 2. **National Bank** — a federally chartered bank to stabilize currency and provide credit (see: [[National Bank]])
> 3. **Internal improvements** — federal funding for roads, canals, and other infrastructure to connect the nation's markets
>
> The American System was designed to make the United States economically self-sufficient and to bind the different regions of the country together through shared economic interests.

---

## The Missouri Compromise (1820)

> [!info] "The Great Compromiser"
> Clay earned his famous nickname by engineering the **[[Missouri Compromise]]** — the deal that temporarily resolved the explosive crisis over slavery's expansion into new territories. He brokered the admission of Missouri (slave state) and Maine (free state) together, and established the 36°30' geographic line above which slavery would be banned in new territories.
>
> It was not a perfect solution — it satisfied no one completely and preserved rather than resolved the fundamental problem of slavery — but it kept the Union together for another generation.

---

## Presidential Campaigns

Clay ran for president three times — in **1824**, **1832**, and **1844** — and lost all three:

- **1824** — Finished 4th in electoral votes; threw his support (and "corrupt bargain" access to his House votes) to [[John Quincy Adams]], who won and made Clay his Secretary of State. [[Andrew Jackson]], who won the popular vote, called this a "corrupt bargain" and spent four years campaigning against it.
- **1832** — Lost to incumbent [[Andrew Jackson]], whom he deeply opposed
- **1844** — Lost narrowly to James K. Polk on the Texas annexation issue

---

## Compromise of 1850

Near the end of his life, Clay again played the role of the great compromiser, engineering the **Compromise of 1850** — a complex package of legislation that temporarily defused the crisis over slavery in territories won from Mexico:
- California admitted as a free state
- Utah and New Mexico territories organized with slavery left to popular sovereignty
- A stronger **Fugitive Slave Act** passed to appease the South

---

## Legacy

- Clay's compromise politics kept the Union together through three crises (1820, 1833, 1850) but left the fundamental problem of slavery unresolved
- The [[Kansas-Nebraska Act]] (1854) undid his greatest achievement — the Missouri Compromise — and the Civil War finally settled what compromise could not
- He is considered one of the greatest senators in American history and a model of legislative statesmanship
