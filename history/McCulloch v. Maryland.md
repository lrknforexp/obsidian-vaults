# McCulloch v. Maryland

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Decided:** March 6, 1819
> **Court:** U.S. Supreme Court
> **Chief Justice:** [[John Marshall]]
> **Vote:** 7–0 (unanimous)
> **Key Issues:** Congressional power to charter a bank; state power to tax federal institutions
> **Outcome:** Upheld the constitutionality of the [[National Bank]]; established the doctrine of implied powers

---

**McCulloch v. Maryland** was a landmark 1819 Supreme Court case in which Chief Justice [[John Marshall]] delivered one of the most important constitutional rulings in American history. The case settled two foundational questions: Does Congress have the power to charter a national bank even though the Constitution does not explicitly authorize one? And can a state tax a federal institution? Marshall answered **yes** to the first and **no** to the second, dramatically expanding federal power and establishing the doctrine of **implied powers**.

---

## Background

The case arose from a direct confrontation between the state of Maryland and the **Second Bank of the United States** (chartered by Congress in 1816 as a successor to [[Alexander Hamilton]]'s original [[National Bank]]).

Maryland, like many states, resented the Bank and its power over the money supply. The state legislature passed a law **taxing all banks not chartered by the state** — effectively a tax designed specifically to strangle the Bank of the United States. The Bank's Baltimore branch cashier, **James McCulloch**, refused to pay the tax. Maryland sued. The case went to the Supreme Court.

---

## The Two Constitutional Questions

### Question 1: Does Congress have the power to charter a bank?

The Constitution says nothing explicitly about a national bank. [[Thomas Jefferson]] and the [[Democratic-Republican Party]] had long argued that if a power wasn't explicitly listed in the Constitution, the federal government simply didn't have it — **strict construction**.

Marshall rejected this entirely.

> [!info] The Necessary and Proper Clause
> Marshall pointed to Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution, which gives Congress the power to make all laws "**necessary and proper**" for carrying out its enumerated powers. Congress has explicit powers to tax, borrow money, regulate commerce, and raise armies. A bank is a reasonable and useful instrument for executing these powers.
>
> Marshall wrote that the Constitution was meant to be a framework for a living government — enumerating great powers and leaving Congress to determine the best means of executing them. It could not be expected to "partake of the prolixity of a legal code" and list every possible law.

This established the doctrine of **implied powers** — Congress can use any reasonable means to achieve its constitutional ends, even if those means are not explicitly listed.

### Question 2: Can Maryland tax the Bank of the United States?

Marshall said no, famously declaring:

> [!info] "The Power to Tax Involves the Power to Destroy"
> If states could tax federal institutions, they could effectively destroy them — and therefore destroy the federal government's ability to function. Since the federal government is supreme over the states (the **Supremacy Clause** of the Constitution), states cannot use their taxing power to undermine federal institutions.
>
> **"The states have no power, by taxation or otherwise, to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress."**

---

## Significance

> [!info] Why McCulloch Mattered
> McCulloch v. Maryland was a constitutional earthquake:
>
> 1. **Implied powers** — Confirmed that Congress's authority extends well beyond the explicit list in Article I. This provided the constitutional foundation for an enormous range of future federal legislation.
>
> 2. **Federal supremacy** — Firmly established that when federal and state authority conflict, federal authority wins. States cannot use their powers to undermine federal institutions.
>
> 3. **Vindicated Hamilton** — [[Alexander Hamilton]] had argued for implied powers and a national bank since 1791. Jefferson had called the bank unconstitutional. Marshall's ruling definitively settled the debate in Hamilton's favor — 28 years after Hamilton first proposed the bank.
>
> 4. **Shaped the modern federal government** — Nearly every major expansion of federal power from the New Deal onward traces its constitutional justification back to the implied powers doctrine established in McCulloch.

---

## Connection to the National Bank Debate

The ruling did not end controversy over the Bank. [[Andrew Jackson]] vetoed the re-chartering of the Second Bank of the United States in 1832, arguing — despite McCulloch — that it was unconstitutional and corrupt. Jackson's veto message is a landmark document in the history of presidential power and states' rights politics. See: **[[National Bank]]**
