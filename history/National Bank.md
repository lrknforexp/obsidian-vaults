# National Bank

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **First Bank of the United States:** Chartered 1791; charter expired 1811
> **Second Bank of the United States:** Chartered 1816; charter vetoed by [[Andrew Jackson]] 1832
> **Proposed by:** [[Alexander Hamilton]]
> **Opposed by:** [[Thomas Jefferson]], [[James Madison]], [[Andrew Jackson]]
> **Key Court Case:** [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] (1819) — upheld constitutionality of the bank

---

The **National Bank** was a federally chartered bank proposed by [[Alexander Hamilton]] and at the center of one of the most bitter political debates in early American history. Supporters argued it was essential for economic stability; opponents called it unconstitutional and a corrupt tool of the wealthy. The debate over the National Bank defined the split between the [[Federalist Party]] and the [[Democratic-Republican Party]] — and later became the central issue of [[Andrew Jackson]]'s presidency.

---

## Hamilton's Proposal

> [!info] Why Hamilton Wanted a Bank
> [[Alexander Hamilton]], as Secretary of the Treasury, argued that a national bank was essential to:
> - **Stabilize the currency** — create a reliable national currency backed by the bank's reserves
> - **Provide government credit** — give the government a place to deposit funds and take out loans
> - **Encourage commerce** — provide credit to businesses and entrepreneurs
> - **Establish national creditworthiness** — demonstrate to foreign creditors that the U.S. government was financially sound

---

## The Constitutional Debate

> [!info] Hamilton vs. Jefferson
> The bank proposal triggered the defining constitutional debate of the founding era:
>
> **Hamilton's argument (loose construction):**
> The Constitution's "necessary and proper" clause gave Congress the power to do anything *reasonably necessary* to carry out its enumerated powers. Managing money and commerce made a bank "necessary and proper."
>
> **Jefferson's argument (strict construction):**
> The Constitution gives Congress only explicitly listed powers. Creating a bank is **not** in the list. Therefore, the bank is unconstitutional.
>
> President [[George Washington]] sided with Hamilton. The First Bank of the United States was chartered in 1791 for 20 years.

---

## First Bank of the United States (1791–1811)

The First Bank functioned successfully for two decades — stabilizing currency, providing government financing, and restraining inflationary state bank lending. Its 20-year charter expired in 1811. Congress, now controlled by Democratic-Republicans, refused to recharter it. Within two years, the financial chaos of the [[War of 1812]] made everyone regret this decision.

---

## Second Bank of the United States (1816–1836)

Congress chartered the **Second Bank** in 1816, again for 20 years. It was more powerful than the First Bank and successfully stabilized the post-war economy — but it also made enemies.

---

## McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

> [!info] The Supreme Court Weighs In
> Maryland tried to tax the Second Bank's Baltimore branch out of existence. The Supreme Court under Chief Justice [[John Marshall]] ruled in **[[McCulloch v. Maryland]]**:
> 1. The bank was **constitutional** — the "necessary and proper" clause gave Congress implied powers, and creating a bank was a reasonable means of exercising its financial powers (Hamilton vindicated)
> 2. Maryland could **not** tax the federal bank — "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," and states cannot destroy the federal government's instruments

---

## Andrew Jackson and the Bank War

The Second Bank's charter came up for renewal in 1832 — four years before it expired — in a deliberate political maneuver by Jackson's opponents.

> [!info] Jackson's Veto (1832)
> President [[Andrew Jackson]] **vetoed** the recharter bill in one of the most famous presidential veto messages in American history, declaring the bank:
> - Unconstitutional (ignoring *McCulloch v. Maryland*)
> - A corrupt monopoly serving wealthy elites and foreign investors
> - Incompatible with democratic equality
>
> Jackson won reelection in 1832 using opposition to the bank as his central campaign issue. He then proceeded to destroy the bank before its charter expired by withdrawing all federal deposits and distributing them to state ("pet") banks.
>
> The collapse of the Second Bank contributed to the severe economic depression known as the **Panic of 1837**.

---

## Legacy

The National Bank debate established enduring questions in American political life:
- How far do Congress's **implied powers** extend?
- What is the relationship between federal financial power and democratic equality?
- The U.S. did not have a central bank again until the **Federal Reserve** was created in 1913
