# Salutary Neglect

> [!info] Definition
> **Salutary Neglect** was Britain's unofficial policy (roughly 1607–1763) of loosely enforcing trade laws and largely allowing the American colonies to govern themselves, as long as the colonies remained profitable and loyal.

---

**Salutary Neglect** refers to the long period during which Britain did not strictly enforce the **[[Navigation Acts]]** and other colonial regulations — allowing the colonies to develop their own governing institutions, trade patterns, and political cultures with minimal interference from London.

---

## Why Britain Practiced It

Britain's reasons were largely practical:
- **Distance** — the colonies were 3,000 miles away; tight control was logistically difficult
- **Profitability** — the colonies were growing and profitable even without strict oversight; why risk disrupting a good thing?
- **Political attention elsewhere** — Britain was frequently distracted by European wars and domestic politics
- **Cost** — enforcing the Navigation Acts rigorously was expensive

---

## Unintended Consequences

> [!info] Seeds of Self-Government
> Because Britain left the colonies largely alone for a century, colonial assemblies like the **Virginia House of Burgesses** and the **Massachusetts General Court** developed real governing experience and political confidence. Colonial leaders became accustomed to making their own laws, levying their own taxes, and managing their own affairs.
>
> When Britain ended salutary neglect after the [[French and Indian War]] (1754–1763) and began strictly enforcing trade laws and imposing new taxes to pay war debts, colonists experienced this as a sudden, unprecedented assault on rights they had exercised for generations. The result was the [[American Revolution]].

---

## End of Salutary Neglect

The [[French and Indian War]] ended salutary neglect. Britain emerged from the war with a massive debt and a new commitment to strictly governing its colonies. New taxes — the [[Stamp Act]] (1765), [[Townshend Acts]] (1767), and others — combined with strict enforcement produced colonial outrage and, eventually, revolution.
