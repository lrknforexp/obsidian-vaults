# Trail of Tears

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Date:** 1830s–1850s (peak: 1838–1839)
> **Authorized by:** [[Indian Removal Act]] (1830), signed by President [[Andrew Jackson]]
> **Peoples Affected:** Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Chickasaw, Choctaw (the "Five Civilized Tribes"), and others
> **Deaths:** Estimated 4,000–15,000+ (Cherokee alone); total deaths across all removals far higher
> **Destination:** "Indian Territory" (present-day Oklahoma)

---

The **Trail of Tears** refers to the forced removal of tens of thousands of Native Americans from their ancestral homelands in the southeastern United States to designated "Indian Territory" west of the Mississippi River, carried out under the authority of the **[[Indian Removal Act]]** of 1830. The name comes most specifically from the **Cherokee** experience — in their language, *Nunna daul Tsuny*, "the trail where they cried" — but the policy of forced removal devastated multiple nations across the South and Midwest. It stands as one of the most catastrophic episodes of ethnic cleansing in American history.

---

## Background: The Policy of Removal

For decades before the Trail of Tears, the U.S. government pursued shifting policies toward Native American nations in the East:
- **Early republic** — Figures like [[Thomas Jefferson]] imagined Native Americans would gradually assimilate into American culture and become farmers and citizens
- **Pressure from settlers** — As white settlers pushed into Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, and Tennessee, demand for Native land intensified
- **Georgia's crisis** — When gold was discovered on Cherokee land in Georgia in 1828, Georgia declared state jurisdiction over Cherokee territory and began seizing land, setting the stage for a direct confrontation

President [[Andrew Jackson]] resolved the crisis with a solution that fully satisfied white settler demands: removal.

---

## The Indian Removal Act (1830)

> [!info] The Indian Removal Act
> Passed by Congress and signed by Jackson in 1830, the Act authorized the president to negotiate treaties with Native nations east of the Mississippi, exchanging their eastern lands for territory in the West. The Act presented removal as **voluntary** — but the "negotiations" were conducted under enormous coercion, and those who resisted faced military force.
>
> Jackson argued removal would protect Native peoples from state harassment and allow them to maintain their cultures. Critics — including **Davy Crockett** and former President John Quincy Adams — denounced it as a betrayal of treaty obligations and a moral outrage. See: **[[Indian Removal Act]]**

---

## Worcester v. Georgia (1832)

The Cherokee Nation took their case to the Supreme Court. In **Worcester v. Georgia**, Chief Justice [[John Marshall]] ruled that Georgia had no authority over Cherokee territory — the Cherokee were a distinct nation with treaty rights that only the federal government could supersede.

> [!info] Jackson's Defiance
> President Jackson is famously (though perhaps apocryphally) said to have responded: **"John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it."** Whether or not he said these exact words, he effectively refused to enforce the ruling, allowing Georgia to proceed with removal. This remains one of the most dramatic examples of a president defying a Supreme Court ruling in American history.

## The Five Civilized Tribes

The peoples most affected by removal were the **Five Civilized Tribes** — so called by white Americans because they had adopted many Euro-American cultural practices:

| Nation | Approximate Removal Date | Estimated Deaths |
|---|---|---|
| **Choctaw** | 1831–1833 | 2,500+ |
| **Muscogee (Creek)** | 1836–1837 | 3,500+ |
| **Chickasaw** | 1837–1838 | 500–1,000+ |
| **Cherokee** | 1838–1839 | 4,000–8,000+ |
| **Seminole** | 1830s–1850s | 1,500+ (plus years of guerrilla war) |

---

## The Cherokee Removal (1838–1839)

The Cherokee removal was the most dramatic and deadly episode. The Cherokee had already gone to the Supreme Court; they had developed a written language (created by **Sequoyah**), a constitution, and a newspaper. Many had converted to Christianity. Despite all this, Georgia and the federal government proceeded with removal.

> [!info] The March West
> In the winter of 1838–1839, U.S. Army soldiers and Georgia militia rounded up approximately **16,000 Cherokee** at gunpoint from their homes and held them in stockade camps before forcing them on a march of roughly **1,000 miles** westward to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma).
>
> The march took place largely in winter. Thousands died of cold, disease (cholera, whooping cough, typhus), starvation, and exhaustion along the way. Estimates of Cherokee deaths range from **4,000 to 8,000** — roughly one-quarter of the entire nation.

---

## The Seminole Resistance

The Seminole of Florida refused to leave. Under the leadership of **Osceola**, they fought the **Second Seminole War** (1835–1842) — one of the longest, costliest, and most difficult wars the U.S. ever fought against a Native nation. The U.S. spent approximately $40 million and lost 1,500 soldiers. Osceola was captured under a flag of truce and died in prison. Most Seminoles were eventually removed, but a small group retreated into the Everglades and was never conquered. Their descendants remain in Florida today.

---

## Legacy

The Trail of Tears is a defining episode in American history:
- It represented a **fundamental betrayal** of treaty obligations and of the Supreme Court's authority
- It demonstrated that the rights of Native peoples would be subordinated to the desires of white settlers whenever the two came into conflict
- It established "Indian Territory" (Oklahoma) as a dumping ground for displaced eastern nations — territory that was itself later opened to white settlement in the 1890s
- It is recognized today as an act of **ethnic cleansing** and a **genocide** by many historians and tribal nations
