# Embargo Act

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Passed:** December 22, 1807
> **Signed by:** President [[Thomas Jefferson]]
> **Duration:** December 1807 – March 1809 (repealed by the Non-Intercourse Act)
> **Purpose:** Pressure Britain and France to respect American neutral shipping rights during the Napoleonic Wars
> **Effect:** Devastated American commerce; deeply unpopular in New England; largely failed to change British or French behavior

---

The **Embargo Act** of 1807 was a sweeping trade restriction signed by President **[[Thomas Jefferson]]** that banned all American ships from trading with foreign nations. It was the most dramatic exercise of federal economic power in the early republic and stands as one of the great ironies of Jefferson's presidency: the champion of limited government and states' rights imposed one of the most expansive and intrusive federal regulations in American history — and it failed.

---

## Background: The Impressment Crisis

By 1807, the United States was caught between two warring superpowers — **Britain** and **Napoleonic France** — each of which was seizing American merchant ships to prevent trade with the other.

The crisis came to a head with the **Chesapeake–Leopard Affair** (June 1807):

> [!info] The Chesapeake Affair
> The British warship HMS *Leopard* fired on and boarded the U.S. Navy frigate USS *Chesapeake* in American territorial waters, impressing (kidnapping) four sailors claimed to be British deserters. One was actually British; three were American citizens.
>
> The attack on a U.S. Navy vessel in American waters outraged the country. War fever gripped the nation. Jefferson, believing the United States was not yet strong enough to fight Britain, sought an alternative to war — economic pressure.

---

## The Embargo

Jefferson's solution was the Embargo Act: a **total ban on American exports to any foreign nation** and a prohibition on American ships entering foreign ports. The theory was that Britain and France were both economically dependent on American trade goods and neutral shipping. Deny them that trade, and they would be forced to respect American rights.

> [!info] What the Embargo Did
> - Banned all U.S. ships from sailing to foreign ports
> - Banned export of American goods to any foreign destination
> - Required coastal traders to post bond ensuring their goods would remain in the United States
> - Later amendments tried to close loopholes as smugglers evaded the law through Canada and Spanish Florida

---

## The Embargo's Failure

The Embargo was a catastrophic economic and diplomatic failure:

> [!info] Economic Devastation
> - American exports fell from approximately **$108 million** in 1807 to **$22 million** in 1808 — a drop of nearly 80%
> - New England's maritime economy — fishing, shipping, trade — was devastated; unemployment soared in port cities
> - Southern planters could not export cotton or tobacco
> - Britain and France found alternative suppliers and were barely inconvenienced
> - Smuggling across the Canadian border became rampant

The embargo hurt Americans far more than it hurt Britain or France. Jefferson's administration tried to enforce it with increasingly draconian measures — including allowing customs officials to seize goods on mere suspicion of intended export — that horrified New Englanders and seemed like exactly the kind of government tyranny Jefferson had always opposed.

---

## Political Consequences

> [!info] The Embargo's Political Damage
> - New England Federalists denounced the embargo as unconstitutional tyranny — they called it "O-grab-me" (embargo spelled backward)
> - The embargo revived the nearly defunct [[Federalist Party]] in New England, where opposition to the policy was intense
> - It was Jefferson's greatest domestic political failure, undermining his popularity in his final year in office
> - **James Madison**, Jefferson's chosen successor, inherited the crisis and eventually led the country into the [[War of 1812]] after less drastic alternatives — including the Non-Intercourse Act and Macon's Bill No. 2 — also failed

---

## Repeal

Congress repealed the Embargo Act on March 1, 1809 — just three days before Jefferson left office — replacing it with the **Non-Intercourse Act**, which reopened trade with all nations except Britain and France. This was also largely ineffective.

---

## The Irony of Jefferson's Embargo

The Embargo Act stands as a monument to the contradiction at the heart of Jefferson's presidency:

> [!info] Jefferson's Contradiction
> Jefferson had built his entire political career on the principle of limited federal government — he had denounced [[Alexander Hamilton]]'s [[National Bank]] as an unconstitutional overreach, and he had opposed federal power at almost every turn. Yet the Embargo Act was arguably the most intrusive exercise of federal economic power the United States had yet seen, requiring unprecedented surveillance of American merchants and seizing goods without due process.
>
> The episode illustrated what Jefferson himself privately acknowledged: that the demands of governance often force leaders to act in ways that contradict their stated principles. The same tension had appeared with the [[Louisiana Purchase]] (where Jefferson stretched the Constitution he claimed to interpret strictly) and would appear throughout the antebellum period as politicians grappled with the realities of power.
