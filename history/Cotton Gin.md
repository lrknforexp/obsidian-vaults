# Cotton Gin

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Invented:** 1793
> **Inventor:** Eli Whitney
> **Purpose:** Mechanically separate cotton fibers from their seeds
> **Impact:** Transformed Southern agriculture; massively expanded the demand for enslaved labor; entrenched slavery in the United States

---

The **cotton gin** (short for "cotton engine") was a mechanical device invented by **Eli Whitney** in **1793** that rapidly separated cotton fibers from their seeds — a process that had previously required enormous amounts of hand labor. The invention is one of the most consequential in American history, but its consequences were deeply contradictory: it made cotton production vastly more profitable, transformed the American South into the world's dominant cotton supplier, and dramatically **expanded and entrenched the institution of slavery** at a moment when it had been gradually declining.

---

## Background: Cotton Before the Gin

By the 1790s, cotton was a promising crop in the American South, but processing it was an enormous bottleneck. Short-staple cotton — which grew across most of the South — had seeds deeply embedded in the fiber that were extremely difficult and time-consuming to remove by hand. A single enslaved person could clean only about one pound of cotton per day.

The result was that despite fertile land and high demand from British textile mills (which had been industrialized by the early stages of the Industrial Revolution), Southern cotton production remained limited.

---

## How It Worked

> [!info] Whitney's Design
> Whitney's cotton gin used a rotating drum fitted with wire teeth that pulled cotton fibers through a mesh screen. The screen's gaps were too small for the seeds to pass through, so the teeth stripped the fibers clean. A rotating brush removed the cleaned fiber from the teeth. The device could be hand-cranked or powered by horse or water.
>
> A single gin could clean **fifty pounds of cotton per day** — roughly fifty times the output of hand cleaning. Larger, horse-powered versions could process even more.

---

## Immediate Economic Effects

The cotton gin triggered an agricultural revolution in the South:

> [!info] The Cotton Boom
> - Cotton production in the U.S. soared from roughly **750,000 pounds** in 1793 to **35 million pounds** by 1800 — a nearly 50-fold increase in seven years
> - By 1860, the American South produced approximately **75% of the world's cotton supply**
> - Cotton became the dominant American export, accounting for more than half of all U.S. export earnings before the Civil War
> - The phrase **"King Cotton"** captured the crop's economic dominance and Southern planters' political reliance on it

---

## The Catastrophic Consequence: Expansion of Slavery

The cotton gin's most profound and devastating consequence was the massive expansion of American slavery.

> [!info] Why the Gin Expanded Slavery
> The gin solved the processing bottleneck — but it did nothing to reduce the labor needed to **grow and harvest** cotton. In fact, by making cotton far more profitable, it dramatically increased demand for the plantation-scale field labor that only enslaved people provided under the Southern economic system.
>
> The result was catastrophic for enslaved people:
> - The enslaved population of the United States grew from approximately **700,000** in 1790 to nearly **4 million** by 1860
> - The **domestic slave trade** exploded as planters in the Deep South (Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana) bought enslaved people from the upper South (Virginia, Maryland)
> - Slavery, which some Founders had hoped would gradually die out, became more economically entrenched and politically defended than ever
> - Cotton profits gave Southern slaveholders the wealth and political power to defend slavery aggressively in Congress and in national politics

The cotton gin is therefore a central cause of the intensification of slavery in America and, ultimately, of the tensions that led to the Civil War.

---

## Whitney's Patent Problem

Whitney patented his gin in 1794, but the design was so simple that it was easily copied. Planters across the South built their own gins without paying royalties. Whitney spent years in costly litigation trying to enforce his patent, largely unsuccessfully. He made very little money from his most famous invention.

---

## Eli Whitney's Other Contribution: Interchangeable Parts

> [!info] Interchangeable Parts
> Whitney's later contribution to American industry may have been even more significant than the cotton gin. In the late 1790s, he won a government contract to manufacture muskets and developed the concept of **interchangeable parts** — manufacturing components to uniform specifications so that any part could fit any weapon. This made mass production and repair dramatically easier and became a foundational principle of American manufacturing. See: **[[Market Revolution]]**
