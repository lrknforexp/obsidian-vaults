## Mercantilism and British Colonial Policy

### What is Mercantilism?

> [!info] Definition: Mercantilism
> **[[Mercantilism]]** is the economic theory that a nation's wealth depends on accumulating gold and silver, and that all nations are in competition to become the wealthiest. A country must be **self-sufficient**, exporting more than it imports.

Great Britain's primary interest in establishing American colonies was driven by mercantilist logic:
- America provided **raw materials** (timber, tobacco, furs, cotton)
- America served as a **captive market** for British manufactured goods
- Colonists were expected to buy British and export to Britain — **not to competitors**

### Salutary Neglect

In the early 1700s, Britain practiced a policy of **[[salutary neglect]]** — loosening enforcement of trade laws and largely leaving the colonies to govern themselves. The unintended consequence:

> [!info] Consequences of Salutary Neglect
> Because Britain left the colonies largely alone, **colonial leaders learned to govern themselves**. This experience of self-governance would become a major factor in the push for independence later in the century.

### The Navigation Acts

When Britain discovered that colonists were selling raw materials to Spain, France, and Holland — **the competition** — Parliament passed the **[[Navigation Acts]]**, a series of laws restricting colonial trade.

- Some colonists resented the acts and **continued to smuggle** goods to and from foreign markets
- British attempts to enforce the acts after decades of neglect sparked enormous colonial resentment

## The French and Indian War (1754–1763)

The **[[French and Indian War]]** (1754–1763) was the North American theater of the larger global conflict known as the **[[Seven Years' War]]**. It was fought primarily between **Britain** and **France**, with various [[Native American]] tribes allying with both sides.

### Causes

| Cause | Details |
|---|---|
| **Territorial Rivalry** | Both France and Britain sought to expand into North American territories, particularly the [[Ohio River Valley]] |
| **Economic Interests** | The [[fur trade]] was enormously profitable; control of trade routes was a major prize |
| **Native Alliances** | Native American tribes formed alliances based on existing trade relationships and mutual interest |

> [!info] The Ohio River Valley
> The **[[Ohio River Valley]]** was the central flashpoint of the war — a strategically vital region that both France and Britain claimed. Control of it meant control of westward expansion and the lucrative interior trade.

---

## Effects of the War

### Treaty of Paris (1763)

> [!info] Treaty of Paris, 1763
> The **[[Treaty of Paris (1763)]]** formally ended the French and Indian War. France was forced to **cede most of its North American territories to Britain**, dramatically reshaping the colonial map of the continent.

### British Debt

The war left Britain with a **massive national debt**. To pay it down, the British government made a fateful decision: **impose new taxes on the American colonies**.

### Colonial Discontent

The new taxes — including the [[Stamp Act]], [[Townshend Acts]], and others — led to:
- Widespread **protest and unrest** in the colonies
- The rallying cry of **"no taxation without representation"**
- A direct precursor to the [[American Revolution]]

### Native American Relations

> [!info] Pontiac's Rebellion (1763)
> The war disrupted existing Native American alliances and territories. **[[Pontiac's Rebellion]]** was a coordinated Native uprising in response to harsh British post-war policies, which replaced the more cooperative French approach to indigenous relations.

## The Articles of Confederation

After independence, the newly united states needed a governing document. The result was the **[[Articles of Confederation]]** (AOC) — America's first constitution.

**Timeline:**
- **1776** — Committee begins drafting
- **1777** — Congress adopts the AOC; translated into French after the victory at [[Battle of Saratoga|Saratoga]]
- **1781** — Ratified by all 13 states, eight months before [[Yorktown]]

### Key Provisions

> [!info] What the AOC Established
> - Gave the nation its name: **United States of America**
> - Every state kept its **power, freedom, and independence**
> - Each state received **one vote** in annual congressional meetings
> - **No titles of nobility** were permitted
> - Congress could decide issues involving more than one state

### Weaknesses

The Articles of Confederation were so ineffective they earned the nickname **"the Articles of Confusion."**

| Weakness | Details |
|---|---|
| No executive branch | No president — no one to enforce laws |
| No national judiciary | Judicial decisions were left to individual states |
| Supermajority required | Bills needed **9 of 13 states** to pass |
| Unanimous amendments | Any change required **all 13 states** to agree |
| No commerce regulation | States made their own tariff and navigation laws |
| No tax enforcement | Congress could only *ask* states for money — it couldn't demand it |

## The Constitutional Convention

**Date:** May 1787, Philadelphia

The Constitutional Convention was called to **revise** the Articles of Confederation. After months of debate, the delegates decided to scrap the AOC entirely and draft a new document: **[[The United States Constitution]]**.

> [!info] The Central Shift
> The Constitution fundamentally **inverted** the structure of the AOC. Instead of powerful, independent states with a weak national government, the Constitution created a **powerful central government** with clearly defined — and limited — state authority.

The main fault line at the convention was between **[[Federalists]]** and **[[Anti-Federalists]]**.

---

## Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists

| | [[Federalists]] | [[Anti-Federalists]] |
|---|---|---|
| **Social base** | Wealthy merchants, landowners, elites | Farmers, shopkeepers, working class |
| **Position on the Constitution** | Supported it | Opposed it (initially) |
| **View on government** | Wanted a **strong central government** | Wanted **powerful states**, feared another king |
| **View on the Constitution** | **Loose interpretation** — implied powers were acceptable | **Strict interpretation** — only what's written counts |
| **Key demand** | Ratify the Constitution as-is | Add a **[[Bill of Rights]]** before ratifying |
| **Notable figures** | [[Alexander Hamilton]], [[John Adams]], [[James Madison]] | [[Patrick Henry]], [[George Mason]] |

> [!info] Resolution
> Anti-Federalists ultimately agreed to ratify the Constitution **on the condition** that a Bill of Rights would be added immediately — a promise the Federalists kept.

## Slavery in the Constitution

The debates over slavery at the Constitutional Convention were among the most fraught and consequential in American history.

> [!info] The Central Tension
> The language of **freedom and liberty** that pervaded the founding era stood in direct contradiction to the existence of **chattel slavery**. Delegates knew this — and largely chose compromise over confrontation.

### Key Questions

1. **Should slavery be abolished?** Most delegates feared that attempting to abolish slavery would cause the Southern states to **leave the union entirely** before it even began.

2. **Should enslaved people count toward representation?** 
   - If yes: Southern states would be extremely powerful in the [[House of Representatives]]
   - If no: Southern states would be extremely weak
   - The result: the infamous **[[Three-Fifths Compromise]]**

### The Three-Fifths Compromise

> [!info] Three-Fifths Compromise
> **⅗ of the enslaved population** would be counted toward a state's total population for the purposes of congressional representation and taxation.
> 
> This compromise:
> - Began a long pattern of **accommodating the South** on slavery
> - Granted political power to slaveholders by counting people they considered **property**
> - Implicitly challenged — and then re-entrenched — the dehumanization of enslaved African Americans

Additionally, the Constitution guaranteed that the **[[slave trade]]** would continue until at least **1808**, after which Congress could choose to ban it (and did).

## The Three Branches of Government

The Constitution established a system of **[[separation of powers]]** with three distinct branches, each designed to check the power of the others.

### Executive Branch

> [!info] Executive Branch
> **Members:** President, Vice President, Cabinet
> - President serves up to **2 terms of 4 years** each
> - **Commander-in-chief** of the military
> - Can make treaties with **⅔ Senate approval**
> - Appoints Supreme Court justices, ambassadors, and federal officials
> - Enforces laws
> - Can issue **[[Executive Orders]]** (have force of law within presidential authority)
> - Cabinet members are **appointed by the president** and serve as advisors

### Legislative Branch

> [!info] Legislative Branch (Congress)
> **Two chambers:**
> - **[[House of Representatives]]** — seats apportioned by state *population*
> - **[[Senate]]** — two senators per state regardless of population
> 
> **Powers include:**
> - Impeach the president if accused of a crime
> - Regulate commerce (tariffs)
> - Lay and collect taxes
> - Coin and borrow money
> - A ⅔ vote in either chamber can **override a presidential veto**

### Judicial Branch

> [!info] Judicial Branch
> **Two tiers:**
> - **[[Supreme Court]]** — appointed by the president; has final say on all legal questions
> - **[[Federal Courts]]** — appointed by Congress; handle multi-state cases, cases involving ambassadors and treaties
> 
> **Key power: [[Judicial Review]]**
> The judicial branch can review the actions of the other branches and **strike them down** if they violate the Constitution. *(This power was later confirmed in [[Marbury v. Madison]])*

## The Bill of Rights

The **[[Bill of Rights]]** — the first ten amendments to the Constitution — was added to satisfy the Anti-Federalists who refused to ratify without it.

> [!info] Core Protections
> The Bill of Rights guaranteed rights that **cannot be taken away**, including:
> - **[[First Amendment]]** — Freedom of speech, religion, and peaceful protest
> - **[[Second Amendment]]** — Right to bear arms
> - **[[Fourth Amendment]]** — Protection against unlawful searches and seizure
> - **[[Sixth Amendment]]** — Right to an attorney and a fair trial
> 
> The Bill of Rights has been updated over time through additional amendments.

## The Lewis and Clark Expedition

Though formally part of the [[Louisiana Purchase]] era (covered in Unit 4), the expedition was organized by **[[Thomas Jefferson]]** and is connected to the westward expansion themes of this period.

- Jefferson asked **[[Meriwether Lewis]]** to lead an expedition to explore and document the western territories
- Lewis recruited his friend **[[William Clark]]** to assist
- **[[York]]**, William Clark's enslaved man, also participated in the expedition

### Sacagawea

> [!info] Sacagawea — "Bird Woman"
> **[[Sacagawea]]**, a [[Shoshone]] woman, agreed to travel with Lewis and Clark as a **translator and guide**. She traveled with her newborn son, and her presence was invaluable in navigating relations with Native tribes along the route.

### Results

- The expedition traveled from **[[St. Louis]]** to the **[[Pacific Ocean]]** and back
- Returned to St. Louis in **1806** after nearly **3 years**
- Brought back **maps** of major rivers and mountain ranges, plant specimens, seeds, and living animals
- Opened the door to American westward expansion
