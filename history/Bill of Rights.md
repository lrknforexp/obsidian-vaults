# Bill of Rights

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Ratified:** December 15, 1791
> **Author:** [[James Madison]]
> **Number of Amendments:** 10
> **Purpose:** Guarantee individual rights; satisfy [[Anti-Federalists]] who refused to ratify the Constitution without them
> **Protects Against:** Government overreach into individual liberties

---

The **Bill of Rights** is the collective name for the **first ten amendments** to the [[U.S. Constitution]], ratified in 1791. They were added to address the chief objection of the **[[Anti-Federalists]]** — that the original Constitution gave the federal government too much power with no explicit protections for individual rights. Drafted primarily by [[James Madison]], the Bill of Rights became the cornerstone of American civil liberties.

---

## Background

During the debates over ratifying the Constitution, **[[Anti-Federalists]]** like [[Patrick Henry]] and [[George Mason]] warned that the document created a dangerously powerful central government with no protections for ordinary citizens. Several states agreed to ratify the Constitution **only on the condition** that a bill of rights would be added immediately.

[[James Madison]], initially skeptical, became the primary champion of the Bill of Rights in the First Congress, drafting and pushing through the amendments.

---

## The Ten Amendments

> [!info] First Amendment
> **Freedom of Religion, Speech, Press, Assembly, and Petition**
> Congress cannot establish an official religion, prohibit the free exercise of religion, or abridge freedom of speech, press, peaceful assembly, or the right to petition the government. One of the most litigated and debated amendments in American history.

> [!info] Second Amendment
> **Right to Bear Arms**
> "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed." The scope and meaning of this amendment remains one of the most contested legal questions in America.

**Third Amendment** — Soldiers cannot be quartered in private homes without the owner's consent (a response to a specific Revolutionary-era grievance).

> [!info] Fourth Amendment
> **Protection Against Unreasonable Search and Seizure**
> Citizens are protected from unreasonable searches and seizures. Warrants require probable cause and must describe specifically what is to be searched or seized.

**Fifth Amendment** — Protection against self-incrimination ("pleading the Fifth"); due process; protection against double jeopardy; no deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process of law; government must compensate for property taken for public use.

> [!info] Sixth Amendment
> **Right to a Fair Trial**
> The right to a speedy and public trial, an impartial jury, to be informed of charges, to confront witnesses, and to have the **assistance of counsel** (a lawyer).

**Seventh Amendment** — Right to a jury trial in civil cases.

**Eighth Amendment** — Protection against excessive bail, fines, and **cruel and unusual punishment**.

**Ninth Amendment** — The enumeration of specific rights in the Constitution shall not be construed to deny other rights retained by the people.

**Tenth Amendment** — Powers not delegated to the federal government by the Constitution are reserved to the states or the people (the foundation of states' rights arguments).

---

## Significance

> [!info] Why It Mattered
> - **Secured ratification** — without the promise of a Bill of Rights, several critical states might not have ratified the Constitution
> - **Defined American liberty** — the Bill of Rights created a legal framework for individual freedoms that distinguishes the U.S. system from many others
> - **Living document** — courts have interpreted and expanded these rights dramatically over time; the 14th Amendment (1868) eventually applied most Bill of Rights protections against state governments as well as the federal government
> - The Bill of Rights has been the foundation of landmark Supreme Court cases for over 200 years
