# Stamp Act

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Passed:** March 22, 1765
> **Passed by:** British Parliament
> **Repealed:** March 18, 1766
> **Purpose:** Raise revenue from the colonies to help pay Britain's debt from the [[French and Indian War]]
> **Colonial Reaction:** Mass protest, boycotts, formation of the Sons of Liberty, Stamp Act Congress

---

The **Stamp Act** of 1765 was the first **direct tax** imposed by the British Parliament on the American colonies. It required that most printed materials in the colonies — legal documents, newspapers, pamphlets, playing cards, and more — carry an official **revenue stamp** purchased from the British government. It produced the most widespread colonial resistance to British rule yet seen and gave rise to the rallying cry **"no taxation without representation."**

---

## Background

After the costly **[[French and Indian War]]** (1754–1763), Britain was deeply in debt. Parliament decided the American colonies — which had benefited from British military protection — should help pay the bill. The Stamp Act was the result.

> [!info] Why It Was Different
> Previous British taxes on the colonies — like the **[[Navigation Acts]]** — were *trade regulations* that taxed goods at ports. The Stamp Act was different: it was a **direct internal tax** on activities within the colonies themselves. Colonists found this distinction crucial: Parliament had never before directly taxed the internal affairs of the colonies.

---

## What Was Taxed

The tax applied to an enormous range of documents and materials:

| Item | Tax |
|---|---|
| Newspapers | 1 penny per page |
| Legal documents (deeds, wills, contracts) | Variable amounts |
| College diplomas | £2 |
| Playing cards | 1 shilling per pack |
| Dice | 10 shillings per pair |
| Pamphlets | Variable |

---

## Colonial Reaction

The reaction was fierce and immediate:

> [!info] "No Taxation Without Representation"
> Colonists argued that since they had no elected representatives in the British Parliament, Parliament had no right to tax them directly. The principle — **"no taxation without representation"** — became the defining slogan of colonial resistance.

**Sons of Liberty** — protest organizations formed in cities across the colonies, coordinating resistance to the Stamp Act. Leaders included Samuel Adams in Boston.

**Stamp Act Congress** (October 1765) — delegates from nine colonies met in New York and issued a declaration that only colonial assemblies could tax colonists.

**Boycotts** — colonists refused to buy British goods, putting enormous economic pressure on British merchants who lobbied Parliament for repeal.

**Violence** — stamp distributors were threatened, tarred and feathered, and forced to resign. Stamped paper was burned.

---

## Repeal

Faced with colonial boycotts and pressure from British merchants losing business, Parliament **repealed the Stamp Act in 1766** — but simultaneously passed the **Declaratory Act**, asserting that Parliament had the right to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." The underlying constitutional dispute was not resolved.

---

## Legacy

The Stamp Act marked a turning point:
- It showed colonists they could successfully challenge Parliament through unified resistance
- It built the organizational networks — Sons of Liberty, colonial congresses — that would eventually drive the [[American Revolution]]
- The constitutional question it raised — could Parliament tax colonists without their consent? — was never resolved before independence
