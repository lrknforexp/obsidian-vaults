# Indian Removal Act

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Passed:** May 28, 1830
> **Signed by:** President [[Andrew Jackson]]
> **Purpose:** Authorize forced relocation of Native American tribes east of the Mississippi River
> **Result:** Trail of Tears and other forced marches; deaths of thousands of Native Americans
> **Court Challenge:** *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) — Supreme Court ruled against removal; Jackson refused to enforce

---

The **Indian Removal Act** of 1830 was a federal law signed by President **[[Andrew Jackson]]** authorizing the U.S. government to negotiate treaties relocating Native American tribes from their ancestral homelands east of the Mississippi River to designated "Indian Territory" west of Arkansas (present-day Oklahoma). Though the act called for *voluntary* relocation and *just* compensation, in practice it led to the **forced expulsion** of tens of thousands of Native Americans — including the infamous **Trail of Tears** — and the deaths of thousands.

---

## Background

By the 1820s, five major Native American nations occupied large territories in the Southeast United States — the **"Five Civilized Tribes"**: the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek (Muscogee), and Seminole. Many had adopted elements of European-American culture (farming, literacy, written constitutions), but this made no difference to white settlers and politicians who wanted their land.

> [!info] Gold and Land Hunger
> In 1828, gold was discovered on Cherokee land in Georgia. The state of Georgia immediately moved to seize Cherokee territory, extend Georgia law over Cherokee lands, and expel the Cherokee. The federal government under Jackson declined to stop Georgia.

---

## The Act

The Indian Removal Act authorized the president to:
- **Negotiate treaties** with eastern tribes, exchanging their lands for territory west of the Mississippi
- **Provide transportation and financial assistance** to relocated tribes
- Guarantee the new western lands to Native peoples "forever"

The language was relatively mild. The implementation was not.

## Major Removals

| Tribe | Removal | Deaths |
|---|---|---|
| **Choctaw** | 1830–1833 | Thousands (first removal; described as a "trail of tears and death" by a Choctaw chief) |
| **Creek (Muscogee)** | 1834–1837 | ~3,500 |
| **Chickasaw** | 1837–1838 | Hundreds |
| **Cherokee** | 1838–1839 | ~4,000–6,000 |
| **Seminole** | 1835–1842 | Thousands (resisted in Second Seminole War) |

---

## The Trail of Tears

> [!info] Cherokee Removal (1838–1839)
> The **Trail of Tears** most commonly refers to the forced march of the **Cherokee Nation** in 1838–1839. The U.S. Army rounded up approximately 16,000 Cherokee and force-marched them roughly 1,000 miles westward to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma) during a brutal winter.
>
> Approximately **4,000–6,000 people** — nearly a quarter of the Cherokee Nation — died from disease, starvation, exposure, and exhaustion. Survivors named the route *Nunna daul Tsuny* — "The Trail Where They Cried" — in Cherokee.

---

## Legal Challenge: Worcester v. Georgia (1832)

The Cherokee Nation took their case to the Supreme Court. Chief Justice **[[John Marshall]]** ruled in *Worcester v. Georgia* that Georgia had no authority over Cherokee lands — only the **federal government** had jurisdiction over Native nations.

> [!info] Jackson's Response
> Jackson allegedly said: **"John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it."** (The exact quote is disputed but consistent with his attitude.) He proceeded with removal regardless of the ruling — one of the most blatant instances of a president defying a Supreme Court decision in American history.

---

## Legacy

The Indian Removal Act and the Trail of Tears represent one of the darkest chapters in American history:
- By 1840, nearly all Native Americans east of the Mississippi had been removed or killed
- The "Indian Territory" promises were eventually violated as well — Oklahoma lands were later opened to white settlement
- The act is widely recognized today as **ethnic cleansing** — the systematic forced removal of a people from their homeland
- Native nations have never been compensated adequately; sovereignty and land rights remain contested issues today
