# Lewis and Clark Expedition

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Dates:** May 1804 – September 1806
> **Commissioned by:** President [[Thomas Jefferson]]
> **Leaders:** [[Meriwether Lewis]] and [[William Clark]]
> **Key Guide:** [[Sacagawea]] (Shoshone woman)
> **Route:** St. Louis, Missouri → Pacific Ocean → back
> **Purpose:** Explore and map the [[Louisiana Purchase]] territory

---

The **Lewis and Clark Expedition** (1804–1806), formally known as the **Corps of Discovery**, was the first American overland expedition to the Pacific Coast and back. Commissioned by President [[Thomas Jefferson]] immediately after the [[Louisiana Purchase]] of 1803, it mapped the vast new territory, documented its geography, plants, and animals, and made first contact with dozens of Native American tribes. It opened the door to American westward expansion.

---

## Background

When Jefferson completed the [[Louisiana Purchase]] in 1803, doubling the nation's territory, he faced a fundamental problem: **nobody knew what was out there**. Jefferson wanted a thorough scientific and geographic survey of the new land, and he also wanted to find a viable water route to the Pacific Ocean — the long-sought **Northwest Passage** — that would open trade with Asia.

---

## The Leaders

### Meriwether Lewis

> [!info] Meriwether Lewis
> **[[Meriwether Lewis]]** was Jefferson's personal secretary and a U.S. Army captain. Jefferson selected him to lead the expedition because of his military experience, scientific curiosity, and personal familiarity with frontier conditions. Lewis studied botany, medicine, astronomy, and cartography in preparation for the journey.

### William Clark

> [!info] William Clark
> **[[William Clark]]** was an experienced frontier soldier and mapmaker whom Lewis personally recruited as co-commander. Clark was responsible for the expedition's detailed maps and proved an exceptional leader in the field. He also enslaved a man named **[[York]]**, who accompanied the expedition — the first African American to cross the continent.

---

## Sacagawea

> [!info] Sacagawea — "Bird Woman"
> **[[Sacagawea]]**, a [[Shoshone]] woman, joined the expedition at the Mandan villages in present-day North Dakota in the winter of 1804–1805. She traveled with her French-Canadian husband Toussaint Charbonneau and her newborn son, Jean-Baptiste.
>
> Her contributions were invaluable:
> - **Translator and diplomat** — she spoke multiple Native languages and helped negotiate with tribes along the route, especially the Shoshone, from whom the expedition obtained essential horses
> - **Navigator** — she recognized landmarks in the territory of her childhood and guided the expedition through the Rocky Mountains
> - **Symbol of peace** — the presence of a woman with an infant signaled to Native peoples that the expedition came in peace, not war
> - **Botanist** — she identified edible and medicinal plants throughout the journey

## The Route

| Leg | Details |
|---|---|
| **Departure** | May 1804 — departed Camp Dubois near St. Louis, Missouri |
| **Winter 1804–1805** | Wintered at Fort Mandan (North Dakota); met Sacagawea |
| **Rocky Mountains** | Crossed the Continental Divide with Shoshone horses (August 1805) |
| **Pacific** | Reached the Pacific Ocean, November 1805 — camped at Fort Clatsop (Oregon) |
| **Return** | Split into two parties to explore more territory; reunited on the Missouri River |
| **Arrival home** | September 23, 1806 — returned to St. Louis after nearly 2.5 years |

---

## Results and Legacy

> [!info] What the Expedition Accomplished
> - **Maps** of the Missouri and Columbia Rivers, the Rocky Mountains, and the route to the Pacific
> - **Scientific documentation** of approximately 178 new plant species and 122 new animal species
> - **Journals** filled with observations of terrain, climate, and Native peoples
> - **Diplomatic contact** with over 50 Native American tribes
> - **Proof of concept** — demonstrated that overland travel to the Pacific was possible, opening the floodgates to westward migration
>
> The expedition did NOT find a practical water route to the Pacific — the Rocky Mountains made that impossible — but it provided everything else Jefferson had hoped for.
