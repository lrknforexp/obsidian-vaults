# Federalist Papers

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Published:** 1787–1788
> **Authors:** [[Alexander Hamilton]] (51 essays), [[James Madison]] (29 essays), [[John Jay]] (5 essays)
> **Pseudonym:** "Publius"
> **Purpose:** Argue for ratification of the [[U.S. Constitution]] in New York
> **Significance:** Most important work of American political philosophy; still cited by courts today

---

The **Federalist Papers** are a collection of **85 essays** written by [[Alexander Hamilton]], [[James Madison]], and [[John Jay]] in 1787–1788 under the shared pseudonym **"Publius."** Written to persuade New York voters to ratify the newly drafted [[U.S. Constitution]], they constitute the most important work of American political philosophy ever produced — and remain cited by the Supreme Court and legal scholars as authoritative guides to the Constitution's original meaning.

---

## Background

After the [[Constitutional Convention]] drafted the Constitution in September 1787, it had to be ratified by **9 of the 13 states** to take effect. New York was a crucial and deeply skeptical state — powerful [[Anti-Federalists]] like Governor George Clinton opposed ratification. Hamilton organized a counteroffensive: a systematic, public defense of the Constitution published in New York newspapers.

## Most Important Essays

> [!info] Federalist No. 10 (Madison)
> Argued that a **large republic** is *more* stable than a small one — a revolutionary idea that contradicted centuries of political theory. Madison reasoned that a large republic would contain so many competing **factions** (interest groups) that no single faction could dominate and tyrannize the others. Size was a protection against tyranny, not a vulnerability.

> [!info] Federalist No. 51 (Madison)
> The classic defense of **separation of powers** and **checks and balances**: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary." Since men are not angels, government must control the governed AND be controlled itself. Each branch must have the means to resist encroachments by the others — "ambition must be made to counteract ambition."

> [!info] Federalist No. 70 (Hamilton)
> Argued for a **single, energetic executive** (the presidency). A single president would be more accountable and more effective than an executive council. "Energy in the executive is a leading character in the definition of good government."

> [!info] Federalist No. 78 (Hamilton)
> Argued for an **independent judiciary** — judges with lifetime appointments would be insulated from political pressure and could protect the Constitution from legislative overreach. Hamilton argued the judiciary would have the power to declare laws unconstitutional — the doctrine later confirmed in [[Marbury v. Madison]].

---

## Impact on Ratification

The essays were published in New York newspapers at a furious pace — sometimes multiple essays per week. New York ultimately ratified the Constitution **by a razor-thin margin** of 30–27. The Federalist Papers contributed to the outcome, though historians debate how decisive they were.

---

## Legacy

The Federalist Papers are:
- The most authoritative guide to the **original intent** of the Constitution's framers
- Cited regularly by the **Supreme Court** in constitutional interpretation
- Required reading in American law schools, political science courses, and history classes
- A foundational work of **democratic theory** studied worldwide
