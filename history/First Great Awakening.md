# First Great Awakening

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Period:** 1730s–1740s
> **Type:** Protestant religious revival movement
> **Key Figures:** [[George Whitefield]], [[Jonathan Edwards]]
> **Impact:** Challenged established church authority; spread democratic ideals; planted seeds of the [[American Revolution]]
> **Successor:** [[Second Great Awakening]] (early 19th century)

---

The **First Great Awakening** was a sweeping **Protestant religious revival** that swept through the American colonies in the 1730s and 1740s. It challenged the authority of established churches and social hierarchies, insisted that anyone — regardless of class or education — could experience God directly, and produced lasting divisions in colonial religious life. Many historians see it as a precursor to the democratic spirit of the [[American Revolution]].

---

## Background

By the early 1700s, many colonial ministers and observers felt that the original religious intensity of the Puritan settlers had faded. Church attendance was increasingly rote and formal. The Great Awakening was a reaction — an explosion of emotional, evangelical religious feeling that swept from New England to Georgia.

---

## Key Characteristics

> [!info] What Made It a "Great Awakening"
> - **Evangelicalism** — emphasis on personal conversion, emotional preaching, and passionate religious experience
> - **"New Birth"** — the idea that a transformative, emotional conversion experience (being "born again") was the heart of Christian life
> - **Open-air preaching** — sermons delivered to massive outdoor crowds, not just in church buildings
> - **Itinerant preachers** — ministers who traveled from town to town, crossing denominational lines
> - **Inclusivity** — the message that *anyone* could be saved, regardless of social standing

---

## Key Figures

### George Whitefield

> [!info] George Whitefield (1714–1770)
> **[[George Whitefield]]** was an English Anglican minister who became the most famous preacher of the revival. He made seven tours of the American colonies, drawing crowds of thousands to his open-air sermons. Benjamin Franklin — a skeptic — attended one of Whitefield's Philadelphia sermons and was so impressed by the crowd size that he calculated Whitefield could be heard by 30,000 people at once. Whitefield was one of the first truly transatlantic celebrities.

### Jonathan Edwards

> [!info] Jonathan Edwards (1703–1758)
> **[[Jonathan Edwards]]** was a Congregationalist minister in Northampton, Massachusetts — perhaps the most intellectually formidable theologian in American history. His most famous sermon, *[[Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God]]* (1741), depicted sinners dangling over hell like spiders over a flame — a terrifying image of God's power and human vulnerability. Edwards combined this emotional intensity with rigorous philosophical reasoning rooted in Enlightenment thought.

## Old Lights vs. New Lights

The revival divided colonial congregations into two camps:

| | **Old Lights** | **New Lights** |
|---|---|---|
| **Attitude** | Opposed the revival | Embraced the revival |
| **Churches** | [[Congregationalists]], [[Anglicans]] | [[Presbyterians]], [[Methodists]], [[Baptists]] |
| **View on authority** | Traditional church hierarchy was necessary | Anyone could experience God directly |
| **Tone** | Formal, reserved, intellectual | Emotional, passionate, populist |

---

## Political and Social Impact

> [!info] Why It Mattered Beyond Religion
> The First Great Awakening had profound political and social consequences:
>
> - **Challenged authority** — by insisting that any person could experience God directly, it undermined the authority of established institutions, whether church or government
> - **Created new denominations** — Baptists and Methodists grew dramatically; established churches like the Congregationalists and Anglicans lost ground
> - **Spread democratic ideas** — the idea that all people are spiritually equal before God was a short step to political equality before government
> - **Founded colleges** — Princeton, Dartmouth, Brown, and Rutgers were all founded in the revival era to train new ministers
> - **Planted revolutionary seeds** — the habit of questioning established authority in religion translated into questioning it in politics, contributing to the spirit of the [[American Revolution]]
