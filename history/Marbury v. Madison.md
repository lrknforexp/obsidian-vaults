# Marbury v. Madison

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Year:** 1803
> **Court:** U.S. Supreme Court
> **Chief Justice:** [[John Marshall]]
> **Decision:** 4–0 in favor of Madison (but established judicial review)
> **Significance:** Established **[[Judicial Review]]** — the Supreme Court's power to strike down unconstitutional laws

---

**Marbury v. Madison** (1803) is the most important Supreme Court case in American history. Though the court ruled *against* the plaintiff, Chief Justice [[John Marshall]] used the case to establish **[[Judicial Review]]** — the power of the Supreme Court to declare acts of Congress and the executive branch **unconstitutional**. This power, never explicitly granted in the Constitution, has defined the role of the federal judiciary ever since.

---

## Background: The Midnight Judges

The case grew directly out of the bitter **[[Election of 1800]]**. After losing to [[Thomas Jefferson]], outgoing President [[John Adams]] rushed to appoint as many [[Federalist Party|Federalist]] judges as possible in his final days in office — the so-called **"[[midnight judges]]"** — to preserve Federalist influence in the judiciary.

**William Marbury** was one of these last-minute appointees, named as a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia. His official commission was signed and sealed but never delivered before Adams left office.

---

## The Dispute

When [[Thomas Jefferson]] took office, his Secretary of State **[[James Madison]]** refused to deliver Marbury's commission. Marbury sued Madison directly in the Supreme Court, asking the court to issue a **writ of mandamus** (a court order forcing a government official to perform a legal duty) compelling Madison to deliver the commission.

---

## Marshall's Masterstroke

> [!info] The Decision
> Chief Justice [[John Marshall]] — himself a Federalist appointed by Adams — faced a dilemma: if he ordered Madison to deliver the commission, Jefferson would likely ignore the order and the court would be humiliated. If he ruled for Jefferson, he would be surrendering on principle.
>
> Marshall found a brilliant third path:
> - He ruled that **Marbury had a legal right** to his commission
> - He ruled that Madison was **wrong** to withhold it
> - BUT — he ruled that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 that gave the Supreme Court the power to issue writs of mandamus was **unconstitutional** — because it expanded the Court's original jurisdiction beyond what the Constitution permitted
>
> Therefore, the Court **could not** issue the order — not because Marbury was wrong, but because the law giving the Court that power was void.

---

## Judicial Review

By striking down part of the Judiciary Act, Marshall established the principle that the Supreme Court has the power to **review acts of Congress** and **declare them unconstitutional**.

> [!info] Why It Mattered
> The Constitution does not explicitly say the Supreme Court has this power. Marshall argued it was **implied** by the structure of constitutional government — if the Constitution is the supreme law of the land, someone must have the authority to say what it means and to strike down laws that violate it.
>
> Jefferson and the Democratic-Republicans were furious but could do nothing — Marshall had ruled *against* Marbury, so there was no order to refuse.

---

## Legacy

- **Judicial review** became the foundation of the Supreme Court's authority and the entire system of constitutional checks and balances
- Every time the Supreme Court strikes down a law — from segregation to campaign finance rules — it exercises the power first claimed in *Marbury v. Madison*
- Considered one of the greatest acts of judicial statesmanship in history
