# Eli Whitney

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Born:** December 8, 1765 — Westborough, Massachusetts
> **Died:** January 8, 1825 — New Haven, Connecticut
> **Known For:** Invention of the [[Cotton Gin]] (1793); development of interchangeable parts in manufacturing
> **Education:** Yale College (graduated 1792)

---

**Eli Whitney** was an American inventor and manufacturer whose two most significant contributions — the **[[Cotton Gin]]** and the concept of **interchangeable parts** — shaped the course of American history in contradictory ways. The cotton gin transformed Southern agriculture and dramatically entrenched slavery; interchangeable parts helped launch the industrial [[Market Revolution]] in the North. No single inventor more clearly illustrates the diverging economic paths of the antebellum North and South.

---

## Early Life

Whitney was born in Westborough, Massachusetts, the son of a farmer. He showed mechanical aptitude from childhood, operating a profitable nail-making operation from his father's workshop during the Revolutionary War. He attended Yale College, graduating in 1792, and traveled south to Georgia to work as a tutor on a plantation while studying law.

---

## The Cotton Gin (1793)

While staying at the Georgia plantation of Catherine Littlefield Greene (widow of Revolutionary War general Nathanael Greene), Whitney observed the enormous labor required to separate short-staple cotton fibers from their seeds by hand. Within weeks, he had designed a solution.

> [!info] The Gin's Design
> Whitney's cotton gin used a rotating drum with wire teeth to pull cotton fibers through a mesh screen, leaving the seeds behind. A brush cleared the cleaned fibers from the teeth. The device could process approximately **50 pounds of cotton per day** — about 50 times the output of hand cleaning.

He received a patent in 1794, but the design was so straightforward that it was immediately and widely copied. Whitney spent years in costly, largely unsuccessful patent litigation. Despite revolutionizing Southern agriculture, he made little money from the invention. See: **[[Cotton Gin]]**

---

## Interchangeable Parts and Mass Production

After failing to profit from the cotton gin, Whitney pivoted to manufacturing. In 1798 he won a government contract to produce 10,000 muskets for the U.S. Army — an audacious commitment for a man with no gun-making experience and no factory.

> [!info] Why Interchangeable Parts Mattered
> Traditional craft manufacturing produced weapons in which each part was individually fitted to a specific gun. If a part broke, a skilled gunsmith had to hand-craft a replacement. Whitney proposed making each component — trigger, lock, barrel, stock hardware — to **uniform specifications** so that any part from any gun would fit any other gun of the same model.
>
> This concept — **interchangeable parts** — made repair simple, allowed unskilled workers to assemble complex products, and enabled true **mass production**. Whitney dramatically demonstrated this concept in 1801 by disassembling several muskets before President-elect Thomas Jefferson and reassembling them with randomly selected parts.
>
> Though historians have debated how fully Whitney actually implemented interchangeable parts (his muskets were still partly hand-fitted), his promotion of the concept was enormously influential. It became a cornerstone of what Europeans called the **"American System of Manufacturing"** — high-volume production using standardized, interchangeable components and machinery operated by semi-skilled workers.

---

## Legacy

Whitney's legacy is deeply divided:
- **The cotton gin** accelerated Southern slavery at a moment when it might have declined, directly contributing to the conditions that led to the Civil War
- **Interchangeable parts** helped create the industrial North, whose manufacturing capacity ultimately defeated the slave South in the Civil War
- His work at his New Haven armory influenced a generation of manufacturers and helped establish New England as the center of American industrial innovation
