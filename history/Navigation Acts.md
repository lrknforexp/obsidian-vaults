# Navigation Acts

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Passed:** 1651–1696 (series of acts)
> **Passed by:** British Parliament
> **Purpose:** Enforce mercantile economic policy; restrict colonial trade to benefit Britain
> **Colonial Reaction:** Resentment, smuggling, and eventually resistance contributing to the [[American Revolution]]

---

The **Navigation Acts** were a series of British laws passed between 1651 and 1696 that restricted colonial trade to benefit the mother country. They required that colonial goods be shipped on British ships, that certain goods could only be traded with Britain, and that Britain served as the intermediary for colonial trade with other nations. They were a practical application of **[[Mercantilism]]** — the theory that a nation's wealth depended on controlling trade to accumulate gold and silver.

---

## Background: Mercantilism

> [!info] Mercantilist Logic
> Britain's colonies existed, in the mercantilist view, to benefit Britain:
> - Colonies provided **raw materials** (tobacco, timber, furs, cotton) cheaply
> - Colonies provided a **captive market** for British manufactured goods
> - Colonial trade should flow through Britain, enriching British merchants and the Crown
>
> The Navigation Acts were the legal mechanism to enforce this arrangement.

---

## Key Provisions

| Act | Year | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| First Navigation Act | 1651 | All goods imported to Britain or its colonies must travel on British or colonial ships |
| Navigation Act | 1660 | Certain "enumerated goods" (tobacco, sugar, cotton, etc.) could ONLY be exported to Britain or British colonies |
| Staple Act | 1663 | European goods destined for the colonies must pass through England first (giving British merchants a cut) |
| Navigation Act | 1696 | Strengthened enforcement; created admiralty courts to prosecute smugglers |

---

## Effects on the Colonies

### Positive Effects (short term)
- Colonial ships and sailors were given **preference** over foreign competitors in British trade
- Some colonies — especially those producing enumerated goods like tobacco — had guaranteed markets in Britain
- The acts provided a framework of trade that helped colonial economies grow

### Negative Effects
- **Restricted markets** — colonists could not sell their goods to the highest bidder in Europe
- **Higher prices** — having to buy British manufactured goods exclusively raised colonial costs
- **Profit extraction** — British merchants and the Crown skimmed profits from every colonial transaction
- **Smuggling** — colonists routinely violated the acts by trading illegally with France, Holland, and Spain — a practice Britain tacitly ignored during the era of **[[Salutary Neglect]]**

---

## Enforcement and Colonial Resistance

> [!info] Writs of Assistance
> When Britain began strictly enforcing the Navigation Acts after the [[French and Indian War]], it used **Writs of Assistance** — general search warrants that allowed customs officials to search any premises for smuggled goods without specifying what they were looking for. Colonial lawyer **James Otis** argued before a Massachusetts court that these writs violated natural law and the rights of Englishmen — one of the first legal arguments for American constitutional rights. John Adams later called this moment the birth of the revolution.

---

## Connection to the Revolution

The Navigation Acts were not themselves the direct cause of the Revolution, but they established the pattern:
- They demonstrated that Parliament felt entitled to control colonial economic life without colonial consent
- When Britain began enforcing them strictly after 1763 and added new taxes like the [[Stamp Act]] and [[Townshend Acts]], colonists already had decades of resentment built up
- The principle of **"no taxation without representation"** applied equally to trade restrictions: why should Parliament control American commerce when Americans had no voice in Parliament?
