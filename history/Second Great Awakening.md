# Second Great Awakening

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Period:** c. 1790s–1840s
> **Type:** Protestant religious revival movement
> **Region:** United States (especially frontier West and burned-over district of New York)
> **Key Figures:** Charles Grandison Finney, Lyman Beecher, Peter Cartwright
> **Impact:** Mass church membership growth, abolitionism, temperance, women's rights, utopian communities

---

The **Second Great Awakening** was a **Protestant religious revival movement** that swept the United States from roughly the 1790s through the 1840s. It was the most significant religious movement in American history in terms of social consequences, producing not only mass conversions and explosive church growth but also launching or energizing virtually every major reform movement of the antebellum era — including abolitionism, temperance, public education reform, and the early women's rights movement.

---

## Background

By the late 18th century, church membership in America had declined significantly. The rationalism of the Enlightenment — the philosophical foundation of the [[American Revolution]] — had led many educated Americans toward deism (a belief in a distant, non-intervening God) rather than traditional Christianity. The frontier West was famously irreligious. Religious leaders feared that the young republic was drifting away from faith.

The Second Great Awakening was the response — an eruption of emotional, participatory Christianity that would reshape American culture for generations. It built on the earlier [[First Great Awakening]] of the 1730s–1740s but was larger, longer, and had far broader social consequences.

---

## Key Characteristics

> [!info] What Made It Different
> - **Camp meetings** — Large outdoor revival gatherings lasting several days, drawing thousands from across frontier communities. The **Cane Ridge Revival** (Kentucky, 1801) drew estimates of 10,000–20,000 people — an enormous gathering for the frontier era.
> - **Emotional conversion experiences** — Participants reported dramatic physical manifestations: weeping, trembling, falling, crying out. The revivals emphasized personal, emotional experience of God's grace over intellectual assent to doctrine.
> - **Democratic theology** — Unlike the Calvinist predestination of older Puritan theology (which held that God had predetermined who would be saved), revival preachers emphasized that **anyone could be saved** through personal decision and faith. This was profoundly democratizing.
> - **Role of women** — Women outnumbered men at revivals and in church membership. The Awakening gave women a new sphere of moral authority, laying groundwork for their later role in reform movements.

---

## Key Figures

**Charles Grandison Finney** (1792–1875) — The most influential revivalist of the era. A lawyer-turned-preacher, Finney used systematic, innovative techniques ("new measures") to produce conversions — protracted meetings, the "anxious bench" (seating for those nearly converted), and direct, emotional preaching. He held famous revivals in the "burned-over district" of upstate New York (so called because it had been repeatedly swept by revival fires) and eventually became president of Oberlin College.

**Lyman Beecher** — Prominent Congregationalist minister who championed both revivalism and moral reform, particularly temperance. Father of Harriet Beecher Stowe (author of *Uncle Tom's Cabin*).

**Peter Cartwright** — Frontier Methodist circuit rider who traveled the backcountry preaching to settlers, epitomizing the democratic, accessible character of frontier revivalism.

---

## Denominational Growth

The Awakening transformed the American religious landscape:

| Denomination | Character |
|---|---|
| **Methodists** | Fastest-growing denomination; circuit riders brought religion to the frontier; emphasized free will and personal salvation |
| **Baptists** | Explosive growth in the South and West; democratic, local church governance appealed to frontier communities |
| **Presbyterians** | Strong in the mid-Atlantic and frontier; split over revivalist methods |
| **New sects** | The Awakening produced entirely new American denominations: **Mormonism** (Joseph Smith, 1830), **Adventism**, and the **Disciples of Christ** |

## Social Reform Consequences

> [!info] The Awakening's Reform Legacy
> The Second Great Awakening produced a conviction that sin could be identified and eliminated — not just in individual souls but in society itself. This belief drove nearly every major reform movement of the antebellum period:
>
> - **Abolitionism** — If slavery was a sin, it had to be abolished. Finney and many Awakening leaders were abolitionists. The American Anti-Slavery Society (1833) was founded largely by revival converts.
> - **Temperance** — Alcohol was seen as a moral evil destroying families. The American Temperance Society (1826) became a mass movement.
> - **Women's rights** — Women's activism in reform societies gave them organizational experience and a moral platform. The **Seneca Falls Convention** (1848) — the first women's rights convention — grew directly from networks built during the Awakening.
> - **Public education** — Reformers like Horace Mann argued an educated citizenry was morally essential.
> - **Prison and asylum reform** — Dorothea Dix campaigned for humane treatment of the mentally ill.
> - **Utopian communities** — Groups attempted to build perfect Christian societies: the Shakers, Oneida Community, and Brook Farm.

---

## Geographic Spread

- **New England** — More intellectually oriented revivals; led to Unitarianism as some rejected revivalism and others embraced reform
- **"Burned-over district" (upstate New York)** — Epicenter of the most intense revivals and radical reform movements; also birthplace of Mormonism
- **The frontier (Kentucky, Tennessee, Ohio)** — Camp meetings; Methodist and Baptist circuit riders; Cane Ridge Revival
- **The South** — Revivals flourished but were carefully shaped to avoid challenging slavery; gave rise to a distinctly Southern evangelical culture

## Contrast with the First Great Awakening

| | [[First Great Awakening]] | Second Great Awakening |
|---|---|---|
| **Period** | 1730s–1740s | 1790s–1840s |
| **Key figures** | Jonathan Edwards, George Whitefield | Charles Finney, Lyman Beecher |
| **Theology** | Calvinist (predestination emphasized) | Arminian (free will, anyone can be saved) |
| **Social impact** | Colonial unity, challenge to established churches | Abolitionism, temperance, women's rights, mass reform |
| **Scale** | Regional | National |
