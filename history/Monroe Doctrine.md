# Monroe Doctrine

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Issued:** December 2, 1823
> **Issued by:** President James Monroe (in his annual message to Congress)
> **Drafted by:** Secretary of State John Quincy Adams
> **Key Principle:** The Western Hemisphere is closed to further European colonization; European interference in the Americas will be treated as a threat to U.S. security
> **Significance:** Foundational statement of U.S. foreign policy; still invoked today

---

The **Monroe Doctrine** was a cornerstone foreign policy statement delivered by President **James Monroe** in his annual message to Congress on **December 2, 1823**. Though it carried no force of law and the United States lacked the military power to enforce it in 1823, it declared that the Western Hemisphere was off-limits to further European colonization and intervention — and that any attempt by European powers to extend their political systems to the Americas would be considered a threat to U.S. peace and safety. It became one of the most enduring and consequential statements in the history of American foreign policy.

---

## Background

By the early 1820s, a wave of revolutions had swept through Spanish colonies in Latin America. Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, Chile, and other nations had declared independence. The United States watched nervously as the major European powers — organized in the **Holy Alliance** (Russia, Austria, Prussia, and France) — discussed intervening to restore Spanish colonial rule.

Two specific threats drove the Monroe Doctrine:
1. **European intervention in Latin America** — The Holy Alliance seemed poised to help Spain reconquer its former colonies
2. **Russian expansion in the Pacific Northwest** — Russia claimed territory along the Pacific coast of North America as far south as what is now northern California, threatening U.S. interests in the region

> [!info] The British Connection
> Ironically, the Monroe Doctrine was partly inspired by a British proposal. British Foreign Secretary George Canning suggested a joint Anglo-American statement warning Europe against intervention in Latin America. Secretary of State **John Quincy Adams** convinced Monroe to issue a unilateral American statement instead — reasoning that it would be undignified for the U.S. to tag along as a "cockboat in the wake of a British man-of-war."
>
> In practice, it was the **British Navy** — not U.S. military power — that actually prevented European intervention in Latin America during this period. Britain had strong commercial interests in the newly independent Latin American nations and had no desire to see Spanish rule restored.

---

## The Three Core Principles

> [!info] What the Monroe Doctrine Said
> **1. Non-colonization**
> The American continents "are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers." The era of European colonization in the Western Hemisphere is over.
>
> **2. Non-intervention**
> The political system of Europe — monarchies and imperial powers — is fundamentally different from that of the Americas. Any attempt by European powers to extend their system to any portion of the Western Hemisphere will be considered "dangerous to our peace and safety."
>
> **3. Non-interference**
> In return, the United States will not interfere in the internal affairs of European nations, nor in their existing colonies in the Western Hemisphere. This was a statement of American restraint as well as warning.

---

## Immediate Reception

In 1823, the Monroe Doctrine was largely ignored by European powers — the U.S. had no navy capable of enforcing it, and they knew it. The actual deterrent was British sea power. Latin American nations cautiously welcomed the statement but quickly discovered the U.S. had little intention of acting on it militarily.

---

## Long-Term Significance

Despite its initial weakness, the Monroe Doctrine grew into a cornerstone of U.S. foreign policy:

> [!info] Later Expansions and Invocations
> - **Roosevelt Corollary (1904)** — President Theodore Roosevelt added to the Doctrine, asserting that the U.S. had the right to intervene in Latin American countries to stabilize their affairs if they could not manage their own debts — effectively claiming a U.S. "police power" over the Western Hemisphere
> - **Cold War** — The Monroe Doctrine was invoked repeatedly to justify U.S. opposition to Soviet influence in the Americas, including the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), the Bay of Pigs invasion (1961), and military interventions across Latin America
> - **Today** — The Monroe Doctrine remains a reference point in U.S. foreign policy debates about Latin America and great-power competition in the Western Hemisphere

---

## Connection to the Era of Good Feelings

The Monroe Doctrine was issued during the **"Era of Good Feelings"** — the period after the War of 1812 when the [[Federalist Party]] had collapsed and the [[Democratic-Republican Party]] briefly dominated American politics without significant opposition. Monroe's presidency represented a high point of national confidence and expansionist thinking that also fueled [[Manifest Destiny]].
