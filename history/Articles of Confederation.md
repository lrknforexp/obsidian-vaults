# Articles of Confederation

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Drafted:** 1776–1777
> **Adopted by Congress:** November 15, 1777
> **Ratified:** March 1, 1781
> **Replaced by:** [[U.S. Constitution]] (1789)
> **Nickname:** "The Articles of Confusion"

---

The **Articles of Confederation** were the United States' **first constitution** — the governing document that held the thirteen states together after independence from Britain. They were replaced in 1789 by the stronger [[U.S. Constitution]] after proving too weak to govern the young nation effectively.

---

## Background

After declaring independence in 1776, the new United States needed a formal governing framework. The [[Second Continental Congress]] appointed a committee to draft one. The result — the Articles of Confederation — reflected the colonists' deep fear of centralized power after their experience with the British Crown. The document intentionally created a **very weak national government** and gave most real power to the states.

---

## What the Articles Established

> [!info] Key Provisions
> - Gave the nation its official name: **United States of America**
> - Every state kept its **sovereignty, freedom, and independence**
> - Each state received **one vote** in Congress regardless of size or population
> - **No titles of nobility** were permitted
> - Congress could settle disputes between states
> - Congress could negotiate treaties and declare war

---

## Weaknesses

The Articles were so ineffective they earned the nickname **"the Articles of Confusion."**

| Weakness | Details |
|---|---|
| **No executive branch** | No president — no one to enforce laws |
| **No national judiciary** | Courts were left to individual states |
| **Supermajority to pass laws** | Bills needed **9 of 13 states** to pass |
| **Unanimous amendments** | Any change required **all 13 states** to agree |
| **No commerce regulation** | States made their own conflicting trade laws |
| **No tax enforcement** | Congress could only *request* money from states — it could not demand or collect taxes |
| **No national currency** | States printed their own money, causing economic chaos |

---

## Crises Under the Articles

The weaknesses of the Articles produced serious crises:

> [!info] Shays' Rebellion (1786–1787)
> When Massachusetts farmers (many of them Revolutionary War veterans) faced crushing debt and tax burdens, they took up arms in **Shays' Rebellion**. The federal government under the Articles was powerless to respond — it had no standing army and no money. The rebellion frightened the political elite and convinced many that a stronger national government was desperately needed.

---

## Replacement: The Constitutional Convention

The crisis of governance under the Articles led directly to the **[[Constitutional Convention]]** of 1787 in Philadelphia. Originally called to *revise* the Articles, the delegates instead scrapped them entirely and wrote a new document: the **[[U.S. Constitution]]**, which created a far stronger federal government.

---

## Legacy

Though a failure as a governing document, the Articles of Confederation were historically significant:
- Established the **first framework** for American national government
- Proved that a government too weak to enforce its own laws cannot function
- Their failure directly shaped the design of the Constitution — each weakness of the AOC was addressed in the new document
