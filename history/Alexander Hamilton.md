# Alexander Hamilton

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Born:** January 11, 1755 or 1757 — Charlestown, Nevis, British West Indies
> **Died:** July 12, 1804 — New York City (dueling wound)
> **Role:** Founding Father; 1st U.S. Secretary of the Treasury (1789–1795)
> **Party:** [[Federalist Party]]
> **Known For:** Economic program, [[Federalist Papers]], [[National Bank]], killed in duel by [[Aaron Burr]]

---

**Alexander Hamilton** was one of the most consequential Founding Fathers of the United States. As the first Secretary of the Treasury under President [[George Washington]], he designed the financial architecture of the new nation — creating systems of public credit, a national bank, and a manufacturing economy that shaped American capitalism for centuries. He was the intellectual leader of the [[Federalist Party]] and one of the primary authors of the [[Federalist Papers]]. He died on July 12, 1804, from wounds suffered in a pistol duel with Vice President [[Aaron Burr]].

---

## Early Life

Hamilton was born out of wedlock in the British West Indies and largely self-educated. He immigrated to New York in 1772 and enrolled at King's College (now Columbia University). When the [[American Revolution]] began, he joined the Continental Army and quickly attracted the attention of General [[George Washington]], becoming one of his most trusted aides-de-camp.

---

## Revolutionary War Service

Hamilton served as Washington's chief aide from 1777 to 1781, handling correspondence, intelligence, and diplomacy. He repeatedly requested a combat command and finally received one at the **[[Battle of Yorktown]]** (1781), where he led a successful assault on a key British redoubt — one of the final major engagements of the Revolution.

---

## The Federalist Papers (1787–1788)

> [!info] The Federalist Papers
> To win ratification of the [[U.S. Constitution]], Hamilton, [[James Madison]], and [[John Jay]] wrote **85 essays** published under the pseudonym "Publius." Hamilton wrote **51 of the 85 essays** — the majority. The papers systematically argued for the necessity of a strong central government.
>
> Key essays by Hamilton include **Federalist No. 1** (introduction), **Federalist No. 70** (arguing for a strong executive), and **Federalist No. 78** (arguing for an independent judiciary with the power of [[Judicial Review]]).

---

## Secretary of the Treasury: Hamilton's Financial Program

As the first Secretary of the Treasury, Hamilton proposed a sweeping economic program that defined American policy for decades and created the first major partisan split in U.S. history.

### The Four Pillars

> [!info] Hamilton's Financial Plan
> **1. Assumption of State Debts**
> The federal government would assume all state debts from the Revolution — roughly $25 million. This tied the financial interests of creditors and bondholders to the survival of the federal government, giving them a personal stake in its success.
>
> **2. Funding the National Debt**
> The federal government would honor its wartime debts at face value, establishing American creditworthiness in international markets.
>
> **3. [[National Bank]]**
> The First Bank of the United States (chartered 1791) would stabilize the currency, provide government financing, and encourage commerce.
>
> **4. Protective Tariffs and Manufacturing**
> His *Report on Manufactures* (1791) argued the government should actively support industrial development through tariffs and subsidies — a vision decades ahead of its time.

### Opposition

[[Thomas Jefferson]] and [[James Madison]] opposed every element of this program:
- The Constitution gave Congress **no explicit power** to charter a bank — the plan was unconstitutional
- Assumption benefited wealthy Northern speculators who had bought up veterans' bonds at a fraction of face value
- Tariffs would hurt Southern planters who imported manufactured goods

Hamilton countered with the doctrine of **implied powers** — the Constitution's "necessary and proper" clause authorized anything reasonably connected to an enumerated power. This debate over constitutional interpretation became the defining fault line between the [[Federalist Party]] and the [[Democratic-Republican Party]].

---

## The Whiskey Tax and Rebellion

Hamilton's excise tax on whiskey (1791) directly caused the [[Whiskey Rebellion]] of 1794. Hamilton accompanied Washington personally when the president led troops to suppress it, viewing the suppression as essential proof of federal authority.

---

## Duel with Aaron Burr (1804)

Hamilton and [[Aaron Burr]] had been political enemies for years. Hamilton had blocked Burr's bid for the presidency in the [[Election of 1800]] (throwing his support to Jefferson) and later helped defeat Burr's gubernatorial run in New York (1804).

> [!info] The Duel
> On July 11, 1804, Hamilton and Burr met at Weehawken, New Jersey. Hamilton reportedly decided beforehand to **"throw away his shot"** (deliberately miss) — a choice consistent with his stated opposition to dueling. Burr shot him in the abdomen. Hamilton died the following day.
>
> Burr was charged with murder in both New York and New Jersey but was never tried. The duel ended his political career and robbed the Federalist Party of its most powerful leader.

---

## Legacy

- His financial framework — national debt, central bank, tariffs — shaped American economic policy through the 19th and 20th centuries
- The [[National Bank]] debate established the principle of implied powers, validated in [[McCulloch v. Maryland]] (1819)
- His vision of an industrial, manufacturing-based economy ultimately triumphed over Jefferson's agrarian ideal
- He appears on the U.S. **$10 bill**
