# Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Date:** 1798–1799
> **Authors:** [[Thomas Jefferson]] (Kentucky Resolutions), [[James Madison]] (Virginia Resolutions)
> **Target:** The [[Alien and Sedition Acts]]
> **Key Doctrine:** **Nullification** — states can declare federal laws unconstitutional
> **Significance:** First articulation of states' rights theory that would fuel debates until the [[Civil War]]

---

The **Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions** were a set of political statements drafted secretly by [[Thomas Jefferson]] and [[James Madison]] in 1798–1799 in response to the **[[Alien and Sedition Acts]]**. They argued that the Constitution was a **compact among the states**, and that states therefore had the right to **nullify** — declare void within their borders — federal laws they judged to be unconstitutional. The resolutions introduced the doctrine of nullification into American political debate, where it would fester for the next six decades.

---

## Background

The [[Alien and Sedition Acts]] of 1798 — passed by the [[Federalist Party|Federalist]]-controlled Congress and signed by President [[John Adams]] — were widely seen by [[Democratic-Republican Party|Democratic-Republicans]] as an unconstitutional attack on free speech and civil liberties. Jefferson and Madison wanted to challenge the acts but had no legal mechanism to do so — the Supreme Court had not yet established [[Judicial Review]] in *[[Marbury v. Madison]]*.

Their solution was to argue that the **states themselves** had the authority to judge whether federal laws were constitutional.

---

## The Arguments

> [!info] Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions
> Jefferson argued that:
> - The Constitution was a **compact** (contract) between the states
> - The federal government was created *by* the states, not the other way around
> - States therefore retained the right to **judge** whether the federal government had exceeded its authority
> - States could declare unconstitutional federal laws **null and void** within their borders
>
> Jefferson wrote these anonymously — publishing them under his own name would have been politically (or legally) dangerous while Adams was president.

> [!info] Madison's Virginia Resolutions
> Madison's argument was slightly more moderate:
> - He agreed the Constitution was a compact among the states
> - He argued states had the right to **interpose** their authority between their citizens and an overreaching federal government
> - He stopped short of claiming states could unilaterally nullify federal law

---

## Responses from Other States

Most other state legislatures **rejected** the resolutions. Northern states in particular argued that it was the **federal courts**, not state legislatures, that had the authority to rule on the constitutionality of federal laws — the position that ultimately prevailed in American constitutional law.

---

## The Nullification Doctrine: Long-Term Impact

> [!info] A Doctrine That Wouldn't Die
> Though the immediate crisis passed — the [[Alien and Sedition Acts]] expired or were repealed — the **nullification doctrine** lived on:
>
> - **1832 Nullification Crisis** — South Carolina, led by John C. Calhoun, invoked nullification to try to void federal tariff laws. President [[Andrew Jackson]] threatened military force; the crisis was resolved by compromise.
> - **Civil War** — Southern states used similar "compact theory" arguments to justify **secession** from the Union in 1860–1861. The Civil War settled, by force, that states could not unilaterally leave the Union.
>
> The Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions thus planted the intellectual seeds of the greatest constitutional crisis in American history.
