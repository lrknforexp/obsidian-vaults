# John Marshall

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Born:** September 24, 1755 — Germantown, Virginia
> **Died:** July 6, 1835 — Philadelphia, Pennsylvania
> **Role:** 4th Chief Justice of the United States (1801–1835)
> **Appointed by:** President [[John Adams]]
> **Party:** [[Federalist Party]]
> **Known For:** Establishing [[Judicial Review]] in [[Marbury v. Madison]]; shaping the power of the federal government

---

**John Marshall** was the fourth and most consequential Chief Justice of the United States, serving from 1801 to 1835. His 34-year tenure transformed the Supreme Court from a relatively weak institution into an equal branch of government. Through a series of landmark decisions, he established **[[Judicial Review]]**, affirmed federal supremacy over the states, and laid the constitutional foundations of American capitalism.

---

## Early Life and Military Service

Marshall grew up in Virginia and served in the [[Continental Army]] during the [[American Revolution]], including the brutal winter at **[[Valley Forge]]** (1777–1778). The experience of seeing the federal government's weakness under the [[Articles of Confederation]] made him a lifelong Federalist and strong proponent of national power.

---

## Political Career Before the Court

Marshall served in the [[House of Representatives]] and as Secretary of State under President [[John Adams]]. He was one of the three American envoys involved in the **[[XYZ Affair]]** with France. Adams appointed him Chief Justice in the final months of his presidency — one of the famous **"[[midnight judges]]"** appointments.

---

## Landmark Supreme Court Cases

### Marbury v. Madison (1803)
> [!info] Establishing Judicial Review
> The most important case in American constitutional history. Marshall cleverly used a minor dispute over a judicial appointment to establish that the Supreme Court has the power to **declare acts of Congress unconstitutional**. See: **[[Marbury v. Madison]]**

### McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)
> [!info] Implied Powers
> Maryland tried to tax the [[National Bank]] out of existence. Marshall ruled:
> 1. The National Bank was constitutional — the "necessary and proper" clause gives Congress **implied powers** beyond those explicitly listed
> 2. States cannot tax federal institutions — "the power to tax involves the power to destroy"
>
> This case validated [[Alexander Hamilton]]'s broad interpretation of federal power and set limits on state authority.

### Gibbons v. Ogden (1824)
> [!info] Commerce Clause
> New York had given a steamboat monopoly on its waters to a private company. Marshall ruled this conflicted with federal authority to regulate **interstate commerce** — the Commerce Clause of the Constitution gave Congress broad power over any commercial activity that crossed state lines. This decision laid the groundwork for federal regulation of the economy.

### Worcester v. Georgia (1832)
Marshall ruled that Georgia had no authority over Cherokee lands — only the **federal government** had jurisdiction over relations with Native American nations. President [[Andrew Jackson]] famously refused to enforce the ruling: "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it." The Cherokee were subsequently removed on the Trail of Tears.

---

## Philosophy and Legacy

> [!info] Marshall's Core Principles
> Marshall consistently ruled in favor of:
> - **Federal supremacy** over state authority when the two conflicted
> - **Broad interpretation** of federal powers (especially the "necessary and proper" and Commerce clauses)
> - **Strong, independent judiciary** as a co-equal branch of government
> - **Protection of property rights** and contracts

Marshall's 34-year tenure shaped the Constitution more than any other single figure — including many of its framers. He transformed the Supreme Court from an institution that had difficulty finding members into the most powerful court in the world.
