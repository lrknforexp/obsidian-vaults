# Sacagawea

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Born:** c. 1788 — Lemhi Shoshone territory (modern Idaho)
> **Died:** c. December 20, 1812 — Fort Manuel, South Dakota (most historians) OR c. 1884 (alternative account)
> **Tribe:** [[Shoshone]] (Lemhi band)
> **Role:** Translator, guide, and diplomat for the [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]] (1804–1806)
> **Also Spelled:** Sacajawea, Sakakawea

---

**Sacagawea** was a [[Shoshone]] woman who served as an essential guide, translator, and diplomat for the **[[Lewis and Clark Expedition]]** (1804–1806). Traveling with her newborn son and her husband Toussaint Charbonneau, she helped the Corps of Discovery navigate the Rocky Mountains, communicate with Native peoples, and secure the horses and provisions without which the expedition could not have succeeded. She is one of the most honored women in American history — depicted on the U.S. golden dollar coin since 2000.

---

## Early Life

Sacagawea was born into the **Lemhi [[Shoshone]]** band in what is now Idaho. Around age 10–12, she was kidnapped by a Hidatsa raiding party and taken to their villages in present-day North Dakota. She was later purchased by or won in a gambling game by **Toussaint Charbonneau**, a French-Canadian fur trader, who took her as one of his wives.

---

## Joining the Expedition

When [[Lewis and Clark|Lewis and Clark's]] Corps of Discovery wintered at **Fort Mandan** (1804–1805) in present-day North Dakota, they hired **Charbonneau** as an interpreter — specifically because his wife Sacagawea spoke **Shoshone**. The expedition desperately needed horses from the Shoshone to cross the Rocky Mountains, and Sacagawea was their only means of communicating with them.

> [!info] Jean-Baptiste
> Sacagawea gave birth to her son **Jean-Baptiste Charbonneau** at Fort Mandan on February 11, 1805 — just months before the expedition's most demanding leg. She made the entire journey over the Rockies and back with an infant strapped to her back. [[William Clark]] nicknamed the baby **"Pomp"** and later served as his godfather.

---

## Contributions to the Expedition

> [!info] What She Did
> - **Diplomatic symbol** — the presence of a woman and infant visually signaled to Native peoples that the Corps came in peace rather than war; this defused potential conflicts with tribes who might otherwise have viewed the armed party with hostility
> - **Shoshone negotiations** — she negotiated with her own tribe (the Lemhi Shoshone) to obtain the **horses** essential to crossing the Continental Divide; she recognized the Shoshone chief Cameahwait as her own brother
> - **Navigation** — she recognized landmarks in her homeland and guided the expedition through the Bitterroot Mountains
> - **Botany and foraging** — she identified edible and medicinal plants throughout the journey, supplementing the expedition's food supply
> - **Interpreter** — she served in a relay chain of translation: Shoshone → Hidatsa (Sacagawea) → French (Charbonneau) → English (Lewis or Clark)

---

## After the Expedition

[[William Clark]] offered to educate Jean-Baptiste, and Sacagawea and Charbonneau later brought him to St. Louis for schooling. Most historians believe Sacagawea died in 1812 at Fort Manuel in South Dakota at around age 24. However, some accounts from the Wind River Shoshone claim she lived until 1884 under the name **Porivo**.

---

## Legacy

Sacagawea is one of the most memorialized women in American history:
- Featured on the **U.S. golden dollar coin** (2000–present)
- More statues in the United States than of any other woman
- Her story became a symbol of Native American contributions to American exploration
- Her role has sometimes been romanticized or oversimplified — modern historians emphasize that she was a skilled, indispensable professional, not merely a passive guide
