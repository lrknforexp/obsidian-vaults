# Senate

## Overview

The **United States Senate** is the upper chamber of Congress, established by the Constitution (Article I) as a deliberative body designed to represent states equally and provide a check on the more populist House of Representatives. Each state, regardless of population, sends **two senators**, serving **six-year staggered terms**.

## Constitutional Design

The Senate was created as part of the **Great Compromise** (Connecticut Compromise) at the Constitutional Convention (1787), satisfying small states that feared being overwhelmed by large states in a purely population-based legislature. Senators were originally chosen by **state legislatures** (changed to direct election by the **17th Amendment**, 1913).

## Powers

The Senate has unique powers: **confirming presidential appointments** (judges, cabinet members, ambassadors), **ratifying treaties** (requires two-thirds majority), **trying impeachments**, and equal power with the House to pass legislation.

## Early Senate

The Senate was intended to be a more elite, deliberative body — a "cooling saucer" for the hot tea of popular democracy, as Washington reportedly told Jefferson. Senators' longer terms and indirect election (by state legislatures) were designed to insulate them from popular passions.

## Key Early Moments

**[[Aaron Burr]]** presided over the Senate as Vice President while under indictment for murder. The Senate was the arena for the great slavery debates — the **[[Missouri Compromise]]**, **[[Compromise of 1850]]** — that defined antebellum America.
