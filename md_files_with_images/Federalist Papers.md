---

The **Federalist Papers** are a collection of **85 essays** written by **[[Alexander Hamilton]]**, **[[James Madison]]**, and **[[John Jay]]** under the pseudonym "Publius" to argue for ratification of the U.S. Constitution. They remain the most important source for understanding the original intent of the Constitution.

---

## Key Essays

> [!info] Most Important Arguments
> **Federalist No. 10 (Madison):** Argued that a large republic would control the dangers of factions (interest groups) better than a small one — countering the common belief that republics must be small
>
> **Federalist No. 51 (Madison):** Explained how the separation of powers and checks and balances would prevent tyranny — "ambition must be made to counteract ambition"
>
> **Federalist No. 78 (Hamilton):** Argued for an independent judiciary with the power of [[Judicial Review]] — the weakest branch needed lifetime tenure to remain independent

---

## Context

The essays were written in response to **[[Anti-Federalists]]** like [[Patrick Henry]] and [[George Mason]] who opposed ratification. They were published in New York newspapers and targeted at New York's ratifying convention — a crucial and closely contested vote.

---

## Legacy

> [!info] Why They Still Matter
> The Federalist Papers are cited regularly by the Supreme Court as evidence of the Constitution's original meaning. They are considered the most sophisticated political science produced in America and among the greatest works of political theory in history.
