# Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God

## Overview

**"Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God"** is the most famous sermon in American history, delivered by **[[Jonathan Edwards]]** on July 8, 1741, at Enfield, Connecticut. It is the defining text of the **[[First Great Awakening]]** and one of the most powerful pieces of early American literature.

## Content

The sermon's central image depicts God holding sinners over the pit of hell like a man holding a loathsome spider or insect over a fire, ready to drop them at any moment. Edwards emphasized that only God's momentary mercy prevented immediate damnation, and that humans had no claim on divine grace. The message was urgent: repent now, because death could come at any moment.

Famous passage: Edwards described the unconverted as walking over hell on a rotten covering, with nothing between them and destruction but the air.

## Delivery and Reaction

Edwards reportedly delivered the sermon in his characteristic calm, even monotone — yet accounts describe audience members crying, groaning, and clutching the pews and pillars of the church, convinced they were about to fall into hell. The sermon had to be paused multiple times because of the commotion.

## Literary Significance

The sermon is a masterpiece of emotional and logical persuasion. It combines vivid sensory imagery, careful logical argument, and urgent emotional appeal. It is studied in American literature classes as the foremost example of Puritan rhetorical style.
