# Shoshone

## Overview

The **Shoshone** are a Native American people traditionally inhabiting the Great Basin and Rocky Mountain regions (present-day Idaho, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming, and Montana). They are particularly significant in American history because **[[Sacagawea]]**, the Shoshone woman who served as guide and interpreter for the **[[Lewis and Clark Expedition]]** (1804–1806), came from the Lemhi Shoshone band.

## Traditional Life

The Shoshone adapted to the challenging environments of the Great Basin, subsisting primarily on plants (pine nuts, roots, berries), small game, and seasonally, bison. Different bands had distinct cultures depending on their environment — some were nomadic hunters, others more settled gatherers.

## Sacagawea's Role

When the Lewis and Clark Expedition reached the Rocky Mountains in 1805, they encountered the Lemhi Shoshone, who were Sacagawea's people. She recognized her brother Cameahwait as the tribe's chief — a remarkable coincidence that secured horses and a guide through the mountains, making the expedition's Pacific crossing possible.

## Later History

The Shoshone faced pressure from westward-moving settlers in the mid-19th century, culminating in the **Bear River Massacre** (1863) in which U.S. troops killed hundreds of Northwestern Shoshone. Today Shoshone people live primarily on reservations in Idaho, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming.
