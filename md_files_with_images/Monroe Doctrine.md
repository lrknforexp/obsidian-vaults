# Monroe Doctrine

## Overview

The **Monroe Doctrine** (1823) was a U.S. foreign policy statement by President **[[James Monroe]]**, largely written by Secretary of State **[[John Quincy Adams]]**, declaring that the Western Hemisphere was **closed to further European colonization** and that any attempt by European powers to extend their influence in the Americas would be considered a threat to U.S. security.

## Historical Context

In the early 1820s, Spain's Latin American colonies were winning independence. The U.S. feared that European powers (particularly the Holy Alliance of Russia, Austria, and Prussia) might help Spain reconquer its colonies or establish new ones. Russia was also expanding along the Pacific coast from Alaska.

## Key Principles

The doctrine had three pillars: **non-colonization** (no new European colonies in the Americas), **non-intervention** (Europe must not interfere in independent American nations), and **non-intervention by the U.S. in European affairs**.

## Initial Impact

When issued, the U.S. lacked the military power to enforce the doctrine. It was largely a statement of principle backed by **British naval power** — Britain also wanted to keep European rivals out of Latin American markets.

## Legacy

The Monroe Doctrine became a cornerstone of U.S. foreign policy. Presidents later invoked it to justify intervention in Latin America, most famously during the **Cuban Missile Crisis** (1962).
