# Urbanization

## Overview

**Urbanization** — the growth of cities as populations shift from rural agricultural areas — was one of the defining social transformations of 19th-century America. Driven by the **[[Industrial Revolution]]**, improved transportation, and waves of **[[Immigrant labor|immigration]]**, American cities grew explosively after 1820.

## Drivers of Urban Growth

The **[[Market Revolution]]** concentrated manufacturing in cities. Factories needed large, concentrated workforces. **[[Railroad|Railroads]]** made cities transportation hubs. **[[Immigrant labor|Immigrants]]** (Irish, German, later Italian and Eastern European) poured into cities seeking factory work. Young men and women from farms also migrated to cities seeking opportunity.

## Early American Cities

By 1860, **New York City** had grown to over 1 million people. **[[Philadelphia]]** and **Baltimore** were major manufacturing and commercial centers. **[[Lowell, Massachusetts]]** exemplified the planned industrial city. **[[Pittsburgh]]** became an early industrial hub for iron production.

## Social Consequences

Urban growth created new social problems: overcrowding, disease epidemics, poverty, crime, and labor conflict. It also created new civic institutions — hospitals, police departments, schools, newspapers — and new political machines (like Tammany Hall in New York).
