# labor movement

## Overview

The early **American labor movement** emerged in the 1820s–1840s alongside the **[[Industrial Revolution]]** as factory workers organized to demand better wages, shorter hours, and safer working conditions. The early movement laid the groundwork for modern labor unions.

## Origins

The first American labor organizations appeared in the 1820s in cities like Philadelphia and New York. The **Workingmen's Party** (founded 1828) sought political solutions to workers' problems. The **General Trades Union** formed in the 1830s, coordinating across crafts.

## Lowell Mill Girls

At the **[[Lowell, Massachusetts]]** textile mills, young female workers organized early labor actions. The **Lowell Mill Girls** staged strikes in 1834 and 1836 against wage cuts and speed-ups, representing some of the first organized labor actions by women in America.

## Early Demands

Early labor activists demanded: a **10-hour workday** (standard was often 12–14 hours), **free public education**, abolition of debt imprisonment, and fair wages. The 10-hour movement won legal recognition in some states by the 1840s.

## Significance

The early labor movement represents the social tensions created by industrial capitalism and the democratic impulse to extend the principles of the American Revolution to the workplace.
