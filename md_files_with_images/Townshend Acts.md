# Townshend Acts

## Overview

The **Townshend Acts** (1767) were a series of British laws that imposed new taxes on goods imported into the American colonies, including glass, paper, paint, lead, and **tea**. Named after Chancellor of the Exchequer Charles Townshend, they provoked widespread colonial resistance and pushed the colonies further toward revolution.

## Background

After colonial protests forced Britain to repeal the **[[Stamp Act]]** in 1766, Parliament asserted its theoretical right to tax the colonies by passing the **Declaratory Act**. The Townshend Acts were the next attempt to raise revenue from the colonies.

## Colonial Resistance

Colonists argued the Townshend taxes were unconstitutional ("no taxation without representation"). **Boycotts** of British goods spread across the colonies. **John Dickinson's** "Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania" argued powerfully against the taxes. The **[[Boston Massacre]]** (1770) occurred amid tensions heightened by British troops sent to enforce the acts.

## Partial Repeal

Britain repealed most Townshend duties in 1770 — but kept the **tea tax** to assert its right to tax colonists. This retained tax led eventually to the **Boston Tea Party** (1773) and the **Coercive Acts**, accelerating the march to revolution.
