# Federalists

## Overview

**Federalists** were supporters of ratification of the U.S. Constitution (1787–1788) and later members of the **[[Federalist Party]]** (1789–c.1820). They believed in a strong central government, broad interpretation of federal power, and close commercial ties with Britain. **[[Alexander Hamilton]]**, **[[John Adams]]**, and **[[John Jay]]** were the movement's leading figures.

## Constitutional Federalists (1787–1788)

The original Federalists were those who supported ratifying the Constitution against **[[Anti-Federalists]]** who feared federal overreach. The **[[Federalist Papers]]**, written by Hamilton, Madison, and Jay, made the intellectual case for the Constitution.

## Federalist Party

Once the Constitution was ratified, Federalists organized around Hamilton's financial program: a **[[National Bank]]**, assumption of state debts, protective tariffs, and close ties with Britain. They dominated the Washington and Adams administrations.

## Decline

The Federalists were never able to build a broad popular coalition. Their association with elitism, the **[[Alien and Sedition Acts]]**, and support for Britain over France (deeply unpopular with many Americans) destroyed their electoral prospects. After the **[[Hartford Convention]]** debacle (1814–1815), the party effectively collapsed.

## Legacy

Federalist ideas — strong central government, implied powers, national economic development — ultimately prevailed in American history, even as the party itself died.
