**Sacagawea** (c. 1788–c. 1812 or 1884) was a **[[Shoshone]]** woman who served as guide, interpreter, and diplomatic asset for the **[[Lewis and Clark Expedition]]** (1804–1806). Her contributions were essential to the expedition's success, particularly in navigating the Rocky Mountains and obtaining horses from the Shoshone people.

---

## Her Story

> [!info] Background
> Sacagawea was born into the Lemhi Shoshone band in present-day Idaho. Around age 12, she was captured by the Hidatsa people and later became the wife (or property) of French-Canadian trapper Toussaint Charbonneau. When Lewis and Clark wintered with the Mandan people in 1804–1805, they hired Charbonneau as an interpreter — and Sacagawea came with him, having just given birth to a son, Jean-Baptiste.

---

## Role in the Expedition

> [!info] Why She Was Indispensable
> - **Language skills** — translated between Shoshone and other languages, enabling communication along the route
> - **Shoshone recognition** — when the expedition reached the Shoshone, Sacagawea recognized the chief as her brother Cameahwait — an extraordinary coincidence that secured horses and a guide through the mountains
> - **Diplomatic signal** — her presence (especially with a baby) signaled to Native peoples that the expedition was peaceful, not a war party
> - **Geographic knowledge** — recognized landmarks in her home territory

---

## Legacy

Sacagawea is one of the most commemorated women in American history, appearing on the U.S. dollar coin (2000–present) and in countless statues and memorials.
