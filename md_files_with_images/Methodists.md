# Methodists

## Overview

**Methodists** belong to a Protestant denomination founded by John Wesley in 18th-century England that became one of the fastest-growing religious movements in American history. Methodist circuit riders — traveling preachers who covered vast territories on horseback — brought Christianity to the American frontier and helped the denomination become the largest Protestant church in America by the mid-19th century.

## Circuit Riders

The **circuit rider** system was the key to Methodist success on the American frontier. Preachers like Francis Asbury traveled thousands of miles per year, establishing congregations in isolated settlements across the Appalachians and beyond. They went where no established church had reached.

## Democratic Theology

Methodists preached **Arminian** theology — the belief that salvation was available to all who chose it, not just a predestined elect. This democratic, hopeful message appealed strongly to ordinary Americans and contrasted sharply with the Calvinist predestination of older denominations.

## Social Reform

Methodism was associated with social reform, particularly early **abolitionism** — John Wesley himself condemned slavery as "the sum of all villainies." Methodist abolitionists played significant roles in the antislavery movement, though the denomination eventually split over slavery in 1844.
