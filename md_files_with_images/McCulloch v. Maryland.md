# McCulloch v. Maryland

## Overview

**McCulloch v. Maryland** (1819) was a landmark Supreme Court decision written by Chief Justice **[[John Marshall]]** that established two foundational constitutional principles: the federal government has **implied powers** beyond those explicitly listed in the Constitution, and **federal law is supreme** over state law when they conflict.

## Background

Maryland imposed a tax on the **[[National Bank|Second Bank of the United States]]**, a federally chartered institution. James McCulloch, the Bank's Baltimore branch cashier, refused to pay. Maryland argued Congress had no constitutional authority to create a national bank since the Constitution doesn't explicitly mention one.

## Marshall's Decision

Marshall ruled unanimously for the federal government on two questions: (1) Congress had the power to create a bank under the **Necessary and Proper Clause** — Congress may use any "appropriate" means to execute its enumerated powers; (2) Maryland could not tax the federal bank because "the power to tax involves the power to destroy" — federal law is supreme.

## "Let the end be legitimate"

Marshall wrote the famous principle: "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the Constitution, and all means which are appropriate... are constitutional."

## Significance

McCulloch dramatically expanded federal power, establishing the doctrine of **implied powers** that governments use to this day to justify programs not specifically listed in the Constitution.
