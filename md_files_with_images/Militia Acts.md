# Militia Acts

## Overview

The **Militia Acts of 1792** were two laws passed by Congress establishing the framework for America's citizen-soldier military force. They reflected the Founders' deep suspicion of standing armies and their preference for a militia — ordinary citizens who could be called up for military service — as the primary defense of the republic.

## Background

The Founders feared standing armies as instruments of tyranny (they had experienced British troops stationed in their homes). The Constitution authorized Congress to "provide for organizing, arming, and disciplining the Militia" while keeping actual control with the states.

## Key Provisions

The first Militia Act required **all free white male citizens** between 18 and 45 to enroll in their state's militia and provide their own weapons (a musket or rifle, bayonet, ammunition). The second act established the president's authority to call up the militia to suppress insurrections or repel invasions.

## Whiskey Rebellion

President **[[George Washington]]** invoked the Militia Acts during the **[[Whiskey Rebellion]]** (1794) to call up militia from four states and suppress the frontier uprising, demonstrating that the new federal government would enforce its laws.

## Limitations

The Militia Acts were largely ineffective — most men never trained, and the militia performed poorly against professional British forces in the **[[War of 1812]]**. This inadequacy eventually pushed the U.S. toward building a more professional military.
