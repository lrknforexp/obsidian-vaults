# Congregationalists

## Overview

**Congregationalists** were the dominant Protestant denomination in Puritan New England, distinguished by their belief that each local congregation is autonomous and self-governing rather than subject to a bishop or broader church authority. They were the heirs of the Puritan settlers of Massachusetts Bay Colony and shaped New England culture, education, and politics for two centuries.

## Puritan Origins

The Congregational church was established by the Puritan settlers of Massachusetts Bay (1630), who believed the Church of England was too corrupted by Catholic practices and that only voluntarily gathered communities of visible saints could constitute a true church.

## Influence on American Education

Congregationalists established **Harvard** (1636) and **Yale** (1701) to train an educated ministry, creating an intellectual tradition that shaped American higher education. The emphasis on literacy (so all could read the Bible) contributed to high literacy rates in New England.

## First Great Awakening Tensions

**[[Jonathan Edwards]]** was a Congregationalist minister, and the **[[First Great Awakening]]** deeply divided New England Congregationalism between "New Light" (pro-revival) and "Old Light" (anti-revival) factions.

## Decline

As New England grew more diverse, Congregationalism lost its established status. By the early 19th century, many New England Congregational churches became **Unitarian** (rejecting the Trinity), a split that reflected Enlightenment rationalism's impact on traditional Calvinist theology.
