# War of 1812

> [!info] Overview
> ![[War_of_1812_Montage.jpg]]
> **Topic:** Second war between the United States and Great Britain
>
> **Dates:** 1812–1815
>
> **President:** James Madison

> [!info] Quick Facts
> **Dates:** June 18, 1812 – February 17, 1815
> **Combatants:** U.S. (+ Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Lower Creek, Tuscarora) vs. Britain (+ Tecumseh's confederacy, Red Sticks, Menominee, Six Nations of the Grand River, Spain West Florida)
> **Causes:** Impressment, trade restrictions, British support of Native resistance, land disputes
> **Key Events:** Burning of Washington D.C., Battle of New Orleans, Battle of Plattsburgh, Battle of Baltimore
> **Result:** Status quo ante bellum (neither side gained territory); Tecumseh's confederacy dissolved; over 22,000,000 acres of Creek territory ceded to the U.S.

---

The **War of 1812** was a conflict initiated by the United States against the United Kingdom and its allies, fought mainly in North America and at sea during the wider Napoleonic Wars. The United States declared war on Britain on June 18, 1812. Although peace terms were agreed upon in the December 1814 Treaty of Ghent, the war did not officially end until the peace treaty was ratified by the United States Congress on February 17, 1815.

- After the **War of 1812**, **Henry Clay** proposed the **American System** — a plan to make the U.S. more **independent** and self-sufficient.
- Being at war with Britain made Americans realize how **dependent** they were on Britain for trade and goods.
- While his plan would fail because of regional tensions with slavery and self-interest, parts would stay alive.

Different regions produce different goods:
- **North:** manufactured goods
- **South:** raw materials for those goods
- **West:** food for both regions

### Market Revolution

In order to stimulate the country while trading, better transportation was needed.
- **Roads:** Roads already existed but were made of dirt. Rain would make them muddy and practically useless; funding went into making roads better.
- **Canals:** Canals were used or created — second-best transportation, using water routes that could connect to other canals, rivers, etc.
- **Railroads:** Railroads were the biggest and best transportation — the cheapest, easiest to build, and fastest.

### 19th Century Immigration

As Americans went west to populate and explore the Louisiana Territory, immigrants populated industrializing northern cities.
- **Irish immigrants** — Potato famine in 1845 pushed approximately 1 million people out.
- **German immigrants** — Farmers looking for work opportunities; Forty-Eighters were political refugees fleeing revolutions.

## Causes

> [!info] Why America Went to War
> - **Land Disputes** — Britain refused to leave territories occupied during the American Revolution, and fighting continued over the northwest territories
> - **Impressment** — Britain forcibly taking sailors from American ships into the Royal Navy; Britain maintained that British-born subjects could not abandon allegiance by serving on foreign ships
> - **Blockade/Trade Interference** — British Orders in Council blocked American trade with Napoleonic Europe, and Britain blockaded French ports during the Napoleonic Wars, cutting off U.S. commerce
> - **Native American Alliances** — Britain encouraged and armed Native resistance to American westward expansion, particularly Tecumseh's confederacy in the west and Iroquois in the east
> - **War Hawks** — Congressional leaders like Henry Clay pushed for war to expand into Canada and Florida
> - **National Honor** — U.S. desire to defend sovereignty in the face of British insults, such as the Chesapeake affair

## Forces

> [!info] American Forces
> - At the start of the war: ~7,000 regular troops, 12 frigates, 14 other vessels, and 515 privateer ships
> - By war's end: ~35,800 troops and 458,463 militia
> - The U.S. Navy was well-trained but small, with no ships-of-the-line; it relied on frigates and privateers for commerce raiding
> - The U.S. Army suffered from inconsistent leadership, poorly trained militia, and lack of funds; militia often refused to cross into Canada

> [!info] British Forces
> - At the start of the war: ~5,200 troops in North America, growing to 48,160 by war's end
> - The Royal Navy was the world's largest navy with over 600 vessels; 85 were in American waters
> - British regulars were better trained than the hastily expanded American army
> - Britain relied heavily on Native American allies — 10,000–15,000 warriors — to compensate for numerical inferiority in North America

> [!info] Indigenous Forces
> - Native allies of Britain (Tecumseh's confederacy, Iroquois) avoided pitched battles and relied on irregular warfare — raids, ambushes, and superior terrain knowledge
> - Native allies of the U.S. (Cherokee, Choctaw, Lower Creek, Tuscarora) provided some of the most effective light troops
> - Indigenous leaders fought on their own terms, withdrawing when losses were too great

## Declaration of War

On June 1, 1812, President Madison sent a message to Congress recounting American grievances against Britain. The House voted 79–49 in favor of war; the Senate concurred 19–13. It was the **first formal U.S. declaration of war** against another nation, and the closest congressional vote for any war declaration in American history. Not a single Federalist voted in favor.

Britain had already repealed its Orders in Council on June 23, but news did not reach the U.S. for three weeks — the conflict was already underway.

## Course of War

> [!info] Major Theatres
> - **Canada–U.S. Border:** Great Lakes/Old Northwest, Niagara Frontier, St. Lawrence River
> - **At Sea:** Atlantic Ocean and American east coast
> - **Gulf Coast and South:** including the Creek War in Alabama
> - **Mississippi River Basin:** western frontier engagements

### War in the West (1812)

American General William Hull invaded Upper Canada on July 12, 1812, but withdrew after a Shawnee ambush and fear of Indigenous attack. British General Isaac Brock boldly moved on Detroit, and Hull surrendered the fort on August 16. Brock was killed shortly after at the Battle of Queenston Heights, dealing a significant blow to British leadership.

### American Northwest (1813)

General William Henry Harrison took command of the Army of the Northwest to retake Detroit. A key turning point came with Captain Oliver Hazard Perry's decisive victory at the **Battle of Lake Erie** (September 10, 1813), giving the U.S. control of the lake. This allowed Harrison to invade Upper Canada, winning the **Battle of the Thames** (October 5, 1813), where **Tecumseh was killed**, ending his confederacy's resistance.

### War in the Northeast (1813–1814)

Both sides fought for control of the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence River. Americans attacked York (capital of Upper Canada) in April 1813, burning its legislature. The British recaptured Fort Niagara in December and burned several American towns in retaliation, including Buffalo. Two American thrusts at Montreal were repulsed — at the **Battle of the Chateauguay** (a force of 339 Canadians and Mohawks defeated 4,000 Americans) and at **Crysler's Farm**.

In 1814, the Americans launched a new Niagara campaign, winning at **Chippawa** but fighting to a bloody draw at **Lundy's Lane** — the war's costliest battle. Britain reinforced with veterans from the Napoleonic Wars but was decisively defeated at the **Battle of Plattsburgh** (September 11, 1814), forcing a British retreat into Canada.

### Chesapeake Campaign & Burning of Washington (1814)

In August 1814, a British force of ~4,500 routed American militia at the **Battle of Bladensburg** and captured Washington, D.C. On August 24, British troops burned the White House, the U.S. Capitol, and other public buildings. The British then moved on Baltimore, where they were repelled at **Fort McHenry** after a 25-hour bombardment. Watching the assault inspired lawyer **Francis Scott Key** to write "Defence of Fort M'Henry," later set to music as **"The Star-Spangled Banner."**

### Southern Theatre — Creek War (1813–1814)

The **Red Sticks**, a faction of the Creek Nation allied with Tecumseh, attacked **Fort Mims** on August 30, 1813, killing ~400 settlers. This became a rallying cry for Americans. General Andrew Jackson led Tennessee militia and allied Creek and Cherokee warriors through several engagements, culminating in the **Battle of Horseshoe Bend** (March 27, 1814), where ~800 Red Sticks were killed out of a force of 1,000. Jackson then forced both hostile and friendly Creek chiefs to sign the **Treaty of Fort Jackson**, ceding most of western Georgia and part of Alabama.

### Battle of New Orleans (January 1815)

A British force of ~8,000 under General Pakenham attacked Jackson's prepared defenses on January 8, 1815 — **after** the Treaty of Ghent had been signed but before news reached the region. The British suffered devastating casualties (291 dead, 1,262 wounded, 484 captured) while American losses were light (13 dead, 39 wounded). The victory made **Andrew Jackson a national hero** and eventually propelled him to the presidency.

## Key Events

> [!info] Major Moments
> - **Burning of Washington D.C.** (1814) — British forces captured and burned the capital, including the White House; the President's House was painted white to cover the burn marks and became known as the White House
> - **Battle of Baltimore** — American defense inspired Francis Scott Key to write "The Star-Spangled Banner"
> - **Battle of Lake Erie** (September 1813) — Perry's victory gave the U.S. control of the lake; Perry's message: *"We have met the enemy and they are ours"*
> - **Battle of Plattsburgh** (September 1814) — decisive American naval victory on Lake Champlain; Theodore Roosevelt later called it *"the greatest naval battle of the war"*
> - **Battle of New Orleans** (January 1815) — [[Andrew Jackson]]'s stunning victory, fought after the peace treaty was signed
> - **Treaty of Ghent** (December 24, 1814) — ended the war with no territorial changes; the war ended in a stalemate with both sides returning captured land

---

## Naval Warfare

> [!info] At Sea
> - The Royal Navy had 600+ vessels; the U.S. Navy had only 8 frigates and no ships-of-the-line
> - American frigates were built larger and more heavily armed than their British counterparts, winning several high-profile single-ship duels (e.g., USS *Constitution* vs. HMS *Guerriere*)
> - American privateers captured ~1,175–1,344 British merchant ships; the Royal Navy captured ~1,400 American merchantmen
> - Britain imposed a tightening blockade of the American coast, eventually extending to all ports by May 1814; this collapsed American exports from $130 million (1807) to $7 million (1814)
> - By war's end, most of the U.S. Navy was blockaded in port

---

## Treaty of Ghent

Signed December 24, 1814, and ratified by the U.S. on February 17, 1815. Key terms:
- All occupied territory returned; prewar boundaries restored
- Americans gained fishing rights in the Gulf of Saint Lawrence
- Provisions to restore Indigenous peoples' rights to pre-war status (largely ignored by the U.S.)
- **No resolution** of the original disputes over impressment or maritime rights — these became irrelevant because the Napoleonic Wars had ended

## Legacy

> [!info] Long-Term Consequences
> The War of 1812 produced a surge of American nationalism, increased foreign respect for the U.S., boosted American manufacturing, and ushered in the "**Era of Good Feelings.**" It ended significant Native American resistance east of the Mississippi (Tecumseh died in battle). The **[[Hartford Convention]]**'s opposition to the war destroyed the **[[Federalist Party]]**. Andrew Jackson's fame from New Orleans launched his political career.

### For the United States
- Surge in nationalism; the "Second War of Independence" narrative took hold
- The Federalist Party collapsed due to its anti-war stance; the **Era of Good Feelings** followed
- Congress passed the "Act for the gradual increase of the Navy" in 1816, authorizing 9 ships-of-the-line and 12 heavy frigates
- Andrew Jackson and William Henry Harrison both rode their war fame to the presidency
- American manufacturing expanded as British imports were cut off, boosting the early Industrial Revolution in the U.S.

### For Canada
- The war became a foundational national myth — a successful defense against American invasion
- Pro-British sentiment strengthened in Upper Canada; immigration from the U.S. was discouraged
- Defense infrastructure was improved (La Citadelle, Fort Henry, Halifax Citadel)
- The memory of the conflict played a major role in consolidating Canadian identity after Confederation in 1867

### For Indigenous Nations
- The death of Tecumseh and dissolution of his confederacy removed the last major pan-Indigenous resistance
- The U.S. negotiated over 200 Indian treaties after the war, displacing tribes from Indiana, Michigan, Ohio, Wisconsin, New York, Alabama, and Georgia
- The British proposal to create an "Indian barrier state" was rejected at Ghent and never resurfaced
- Indigenous peoples are generally considered the clear losers of the War of 1812

### For Britain
- The war is largely forgotten in the UK, viewed as a minor sideshow to the Napoleonic Wars
- The Royal Navy remained the world's dominant naval power
- Britain adopted a long-term policy of accommodation toward the United States to protect Canada
- The **Rush–Bagot Treaty (1817)** demilitarized the Great Lakes and Lake Champlain; it remains in effect today

## The Monroe Doctrine

> [!info] Monroe Doctrine (1823)
> During President [[James Monroe]]'s annual message to Congress in 1823, Monroe issued a warning to European nations to stay out of the Western Hemisphere. This part of his address became known as the **Monroe Doctrine**. Newly independent nations in Latin and South America were seen as vulnerable to European powers who might seek to reclaim former colonies. The doctrine evolved over the years to assert U.S. dominance over other nations in the Americas.
