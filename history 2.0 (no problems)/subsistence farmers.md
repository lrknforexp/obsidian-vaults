# subsistence farmers

## Overview

**Subsistence farmers** were agricultural workers who grew crops primarily to feed themselves and their families rather than to sell for profit. Subsistence farming was the dominant economic mode in colonial and early American rural life, particularly in the backcountry and frontier regions, before the **[[Market Revolution]]** transformed agriculture toward commercial production.

## Colonial Era

Most colonial American farmers practiced subsistence or near-subsistence agriculture. They grew a variety of crops — corn, wheat, vegetables — raised livestock, and made many goods at home. Trade with neighbors and local merchants supplemented what they could not produce themselves, often through **[[barter economy|barter]]** rather than cash.

## Contrast with Market Agriculture

By the early 19th century, improvements in transportation — canals like the **[[Erie Canal]]**, and later [[Railroad|railroads]]** — began connecting frontier farmers to distant markets. Farmers could now **specialize** in cash crops (wheat, cotton, corn) and sell surpluses. This transition from subsistence to market farming was a defining feature of the **[[Market Revolution]]**.

## Significance

The shift away from subsistence farming toward commercial agriculture transformed the American economy, increased interdependence between regions, and contributed to growing inequality between wealthy commercial farmers and poor subsistence farmers.
